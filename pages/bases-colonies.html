What counts as a "base" in stock KSP

A "base" here means a permanent or semi-permanent surface installation — usually combining crew habitation, science equipment, and often an ISRU rig — that stays in place rather than launching back to orbit right away. Stock KSP has no survival-mode life support requirement, so a base isn't something you're forced to build to keep Kerbals alive. Its value instead comes from roleplay, contracts, and science: a well-placed base is a long-term science collection point, a regional fuel depot, or simply a visible monument to a mission that mattered to you.

Life support mods change this

Popular mods like Kerbal Health, USI Life Support, and TAC Life Support add resource-consumption or long-term-health mechanics that make permanent habitation genuinely necessary for crew survival on long missions. That's outside stock behavior — see Tips & Mods for an overview if you want that added challenge.

Planning a base

Before launching base modules, decide what the base is actually for — that answer drives the site selection.

  • Biome and science value: a site near multiple distinct biomes (or a border between two) lets a stationary base or a short rover trip collect a wider range of science reports without long-distance travel.
  • Confirmed ore deposits: if the base will double as an ISRU fuel depot (see ISRU & Mining), site selection should weight a confirmed high-concentration ore reading at least as heavily as biome variety.
  • Latitude and lighting: a site near the equator generally gets more consistent sunlight over the course of a local day than a high-latitude site, which matters a lot if the base depends on solar power. A polar or high-latitude site can experience much longer stretches of darkness depending on the body's axial tilt and orbit.
  • Fixed vs. mobile: decide whether this is a single fixed installation (modules landed once and left in place) or a mobile, rover-based "base" that relocates to chase biomes or ore deposits. Fixed bases are simpler to power and connect; mobile bases trade that simplicity for flexibility.

Delivering and connecting modules

Multi-module bases are usually built by landing separate craft near each other and connecting them afterward, rather than launching one enormous single vessel. That's both more practical to build in the VAB and more forgiving if one landing goes wrong.

  1. Land the first module and treat it as your reference point. Everything else will be targeted relative to where this one ends up, so a stable, flat landing spot matters more for the first module than for any single lander on its own.
  2. Target subsequent landings visually. Precision landing next to an existing surface object is done by eye and careful throttle control during the final descent — burn your deorbit and suicide-burn (see Landing & Return) so your final approach path passes near the existing base, then use RCS or a light hover-descent for the last stretch to walk sideways toward the target visually.
  3. Expect several attempts before you land close enough. Landing multiple separate craft within a short driving distance of each other is one of the more difficult precision-flying skills in the game — budget extra fuel margin on early attempts for a correction hop if you land farther away than planned.
  4. Connect modules with a rover-driven docking port, or leave them structurally separate. Docking ports work on the ground the same way they do in orbit — drive one module (mounted on wheels, or nudged with RCS/engines at very low thrust) into alignment with another module's port to form a single connected craft with shared resources. Alternatively, many bases simply leave modules as separate, nearby vessels connected only by proximity and rover shuttle runs, which avoids the precision-docking problem entirely at the cost of not sharing resources directly.
Terrain ruins more docking attempts than piloting does

Uneven ground near an existing base module is a common reason a docking attempt fails even with good piloting — a port that's tilted relative to its target because one module is sitting on a rock or slope won't align cleanly. Scout the immediate landing area (zoom in during descent, or check with a rover beforehand) rather than assuming the ground next to your base is as flat as where the first module landed.

Power planning for a fixed base

The same power considerations from ISRU rigs apply to bases generally, scaled up to cover habitation and science equipment as well as any converter:

Unreliable sunlightRTGs
Bridging darknessBatteries
  • Solar reliability depends on latitude and day length. A base near a body's equator generally sees more consistent sun exposure across its day/night cycle than a high-latitude site; long nights (common on slowly-rotating bodies) mean longer stretches with zero solar output.
  • RTGs cover the gaps solar can't. Anywhere sunlight is unreliable — high latitude, a body far from the Sun, or a location with unusually long nights — RTGs provide constant baseline power regardless of lighting conditions, at the cost of more mass and expense per unit of power than solar panels.
  • Batteries buffer the base through eclipses and night. Size battery capacity to cover your base's power draw through its longest expected dark period, not just a brief eclipse — a base that browns out every local night will interrupt science processing and ISRU production repeatedly.

Base science strategy

A base's most durable value is usually as a long-term science platform rather than a one-time collection point:

  • Mobile Processing Lab as an anchor module. A base built around a Mobile Processing Lab can convert accumulated science data (and stored surface samples) into additional Science over time, effectively turning a base into a slow, passive science generator as long as it's staffed and kept supplied with fresh data.
  • Repeat visits and rover trips to uncollected biomes. Rather than treating a base as a single collection event, use it as a hub for repeated rover trips (see Rovers & Surface Ops) to nearby biomes you haven't sampled yet, feeding new experiment results back to the lab for processing.
  • Combine with orbital science. A base doesn't have to do all the work alone — pair it with a science-focused satellite in the same system (see Science & Research) so surface and orbital science come in on parallel tracks.

Logistics: resupply, crew, and fuel depots

Once a base exists, keeping it useful is an ongoing logistics exercise rather than a one-time build:

  • Resupply missions. Depending on your rules (stock or with life-support mods), periodic resupply runs might deliver spare parts, additional science instruments, or (with a life-support mod) consumables — plan a docking port or a predictable delivery zone on the base for this.
  • Crew rotation. Long-duration crew assignments are a roleplay and Kerbal-morale consideration more than a mechanical one in stock KSP, but rotating crew in and out via a dedicated shuttle craft is a common practice for bases meant to feel "lived in," and is functionally required under most life-support mods.
  • Fuel depot role. A base with an ISRU rig attached (see ISRU & Mining) can serve double duty as a regional refueling stop, letting other missions in the area top off instead of carrying every drop of fuel from Kerbin — this is especially valuable for a base supporting a wider regional exploration program or acting as a staging point for return trips.

Roleplay building vs. functional building

This chapter is optional — you don't need it to "beat" the game

Base building sits at the advanced, optional end of the game. Nothing about completing a career save or a full tech tree requires a permanent base. It's worth separating two different goals before you start:

  • Roleplay/aesthetic base building: arranging modules to look like a coherent outpost, matching a theme, or just building something you think looks cool. This is entirely about presentation and enjoyment — there's no wrong way to do it.
  • Functionally useful base building: a base built specifically to generate ongoing Science, serve as a fuel depot, or support a regional exploration program. This is judged by whether it actually saves you Δv, time, or Funds compared to not having it.

Both are legitimate ways to play. Just go in knowing which one you're optimizing for — a base built purely for looks doesn't need to justify its ISRU setup cost, and a base built for logistics doesn't need to look pretty.
