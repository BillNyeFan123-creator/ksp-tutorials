Rendezvous & Docking

Meeting up with another craft in orbit and docking with it without panicking or crashing.

Docking hardware

Docking requires a compatible port on both craft, plus fine attitude and translation control to line them up.

Port sizeTypical use
Junior / RadialSmall probes, radially-mounted side dockings, lightweight craft
Standard / Regular (Clamp-o-tron)The general-purpose size — most crew pods and mid-size stations
SeniorLarge modules, heavy fuel transfers, station cores
Sizes must match

A Junior port will not dock to a Standard or Senior port. Plan your whole station or ship family around one or two port sizes so anything you launch later can actually connect.

  • RCS (Reaction Control System) thrusters burn monopropellant to translate your craft directly sideways, up/down, and fore/aft — essential for fine docking adjustments that your main engine and reaction wheels can't do cleanly. Enable RCS with the dedicated toggle (default key R).
  • SAS Target mode — with a target set and SAS on, you can point your craft directly at the target, or at the target's own docking port orientation (target prograde/retrograde and the dedicated "point at target" hold), which is much easier than manually aiming during final approach.

Launch timing and plane matching

Rendezvousing is much cheaper if you launch directly into your target's orbital plane instead of fixing your inclination afterward.

  1. Watch the target pass overhead — in the tracking station or map view, you can see when the target's ground track will pass near your launch site. Launching within a minute or two of that pass puts you close to its plane from the start.
  2. Launch straight up briefly, then turn toward the target's inclination instead of the default due-east ascent, aiming your ascent azimuth to match the target's orbital tilt.
  3. If you're off-plane after reaching orbit, fix it with a plane-change burn at one of your orbit's nodes relative to the target (see Orbital Mechanics for why nodes are the cheap place to do this) — cheaper the earlier and the farther out you do it.

Setting a target and reading closest approach

In map view, click the other craft (or switch to it in the tracking station and target it from there) and choose Set as Target. Once targeted, KSP shows:

  • A closest approach marker on both orbit lines, showing where the two paths come nearest each other and the predicted distance at that point.
  • Relative velocity and distance readouts near the navball once you're close enough or switch the navball to Target mode.

Planning the intercept

  1. Place a maneuver node on your orbit, generally a bit ahead of where your orbits currently cross or come close.
  2. Drag the prograde/retrograde handle and watch the closest-approach distance readout — you're trying to shrink it toward zero (or a few hundred meters) rather than trying to match velocity yet.
  3. Add small normal/radial adjustments if needed to shrink the approach distance further once prograde alone gets you close.
  4. Execute the burn normally (see the maneuver node procedure on the Orbital Mechanics page), then time-warp to the intercept point — physics warp works fine once you're within a few kilometers, since regular warp can be risky that close to another craft.
Aim for a few hundred meters, not zero

You don't need the node to predict an exact collision — a closest approach of a few hundred meters to a couple kilometers is a perfectly good intercept. You'll close the rest of the gap manually during the next phase, which gives you more control than trying to nail an exact numeric zero with the node gizmo.

Closing in and killing relative velocity

This is the phase where most of the actual "flying" happens, and where switching the navball to Target mode matters most — it shows your velocity relative to the target instead of relative to the body you're orbiting, which is what you actually care about now.

  1. At long range (a few km down to ~500m), point at the target marker (or its retrograde, if you're approaching faster than you'd like) and use your main engine or RCS to null out most of the relative velocity, aiming to arrive near the target moving only a few m/s toward it.
  2. Switch to RCS translation once you're within a few hundred meters. Use fore/aft (H/N by default), up/down, and left/right translation keys to drift straight toward the target without needing to rotate the whole craft.
  3. Kill relative velocity in stages — cancel your closing speed periodically so you don't build up too much momentum, rather than coasting the whole distance at a constant speed.
  4. Match orientation — rotate so your docking port faces the target's docking port, using target-relative SAS hold if available, or manual rotation with RCS/reaction wheels.

Final approach and docking

200m+under ~2-3 m/s
~50-200munder ~1-2 m/s
Under 50munder ~0.5 m/s
At contact~0.2-0.5 m/s
  1. Line up the ports visually and on the navball — the target marker should sit directly under your prograde marker as you creep forward, meaning your closing velocity is aimed straight down the docking axis.
  2. Use small RCS taps, not sustained thrust, to correct drift sideways or vertically as you close the last hundred meters — overcorrecting is the most common cause of a missed approach.
  3. Let the ports touch at low speed. A successful capture locks the two craft together automatically (you'll see a capture message and the two vessels merge into one in the tracking station) as long as alignment is close enough and closing speed is low enough — the ports have some built-in tolerance for small misalignment.
Too fast at contact damages parts

Docking ports (and anything nearby) can take collision damage or simply bounce off each other without capturing if you hit at more than a couple m/s. If you're coming in too hot, back off with a retrograde RCS tap and try again rather than letting it hit.

Switching control between ports

Once docked, the combined craft has one active "control point" (usually wherever you last had "Control From Here" set). Right-click any docking port or command part and choose Control From Here, or cycle through control points quickly with the [ and ] keys — useful for reorienting your controls to match whichever end of the combined station or ship you want to fly from next.

This matters more than it sounds: "forward" for staging, RCS translation, and the navball's prograde reference are all defined relative to whichever part is currently the control point. If you undock from a station and forget to reset control to your own ship's pod or probe core, your translation controls may feel backward or your SAS may fight you, because you're technically still flying with the station's original orientation as the reference. After any dock or undock, it's good habit to check which part is currently controlling before doing anything else.

Undocking safely

Right-click the docking port you're attached through and choose Undock. The two craft separate with a small amount of push-apart force built into the port. Immediately after undocking:

  1. Confirm control — make sure "Control From Here" is set on the departing craft's own pod or probe core, not left on a part now belonging to the other vessel.
  2. Apply a small retrograde or radial nudge with RCS to open some distance before doing anything else, since the two craft are still very close together right after separation and any engine burn risks a collision if you don't clear the area first.
  3. Re-target if needed — if you plan to redock later or fly formation, re-select the station (or your departing craft, from the station's perspective) as target.

Troubleshooting

Common docking problems
  • Ports touch but won't connect — check that both are the same size and neither is already docked to something else; also confirm relative speed was low enough at contact.
  • Uncontrolled drift after undocking or during approach — make sure RCS is actually enabled (R) and that you have monopropellant remaining in tanks connected to active RCS thrusters.
  • Navball showing the wrong numbers — confirm you're in Target mode, not Orbit or Surface mode, or the velocity readout won't reflect your motion relative to the target.
  • Approaching too fast and overshooting — cancel closing velocity earlier and in smaller increments; it's much easier to nudge forward again than to kill excess speed at close range.
  • Out of monopropellant — check RCS tank levels before you commit to the final approach; running dry mid-approach leaves you drifting with only reaction wheels for rotation and no way to translate.
  • RCS thrusters blocked by other parts — thrusters mounted too close to fuel tanks, solar panels, or other station modules can have their exhaust obstructed, weakening or disabling that thruster's authority. Check craft symmetry and clearances in the VAB.
Optional aid: Docking Port Alignment Indicator

A popular community mod overlays a precise alignment gauge on screen showing exact offset and rotation between your port and the target's. It's entirely optional — stock alignment is doable with the navball and target markers alone — but many players find it speeds up docking considerably.
