The three currencies

Career mode runs on three separate resources, each earned and spent differently. Understanding what drives each one is the key to not stalling out partway through a save.

CurrencyWhat it doesHow you gain itHow you lose it
Funds Spent on launches, vehicle parts, and facility upgrades Contract rewards, recovering vessels/stages Launch costs, buying parts, some contract penalties
Science Unlocks new parts in the tech tree Running experiments, transmitting/recovering data, contracts Not directly lost, but can be traded away via strategies
Reputation Affects which contracts are offered and their reward size Completing contracts, mission milestones Failed contracts, Kerbal deaths, mission failures
Reputation is easy to overlook, and it compounds

Low Reputation doesn't just look bad — it directly shrinks both the number and quality of contracts Mission Control offers you, which slows your Funds and Science income at the same time. Avoiding needless Kerbal deaths and mission failures pays off well beyond the immediate contract.

Contracts in depth

Contracts are the main structured source of Funds, Science, and Reputation in a career save. A few mechanics are worth understanding before you start accepting them freely:

  • Accepting a contract commits you to its terms but doesn't cost anything upfront in stock rules (some contracts do include an advance-payment or deadline structure — read the terms before accepting).
  • Milestones are the individual sub-goals within a single contract (e.g. "launch a vessel," then "reach orbit," then "return safely") — many contracts pay out partial rewards as you complete milestones, not only at final completion, which is useful if you're not sure you'll finish the whole thing.
  • Rewards typically include some mix of Funds, Science, and Reputation, weighted toward whichever purpose the contract serves — a "first orbit" style contract usually rewards Science more heavily, while multi-satellite or logistics contracts skew toward Funds.
  • Penalties apply mainly to failure or expiration on time-limited contracts, and usually cost a mix of Funds and Reputation — factor a contract's deadline into your acceptance decision if it requires a mission profile you're not ready to fly yet.
  • Prioritize contracts that match a mission you were already planning. The efficient way to play career mode isn't to chase every contract available — it's to accept the ones that align with a mission profile you'd have flown anyway (a Mun landing, a satellite network, a rescue), turning a mission you wanted to do into one that also pays.

KSC facility upgrades

Space Center facilities each have their own upgrade levels, and upgrading them raises specific caps rather than granting a general bonus. Knowing what each one actually raises helps you prioritize Funds toward the upgrade that's currently blocking you, instead of upgrading whatever's cheapest.

FacilityWhat upgrading it raises
VAB / SPHMaximum part count and craft size/mass you can build
Launch Pad / RunwayMaximum craft size and mass you can launch
Tracking StationTracking range and the number of trackable objects/maneuver nodes
Astronaut ComplexKerbal recruitment and training slots
Mission ControlNumber of active contracts and available strategy slots
R&DScience curve efficiency and access to further tech tree tiers
AdministrationNumber of active strategies and their effectiveness

A sensible early-game upgrade priority

  1. Mission Control first (or early). More active contract slots means more simultaneous income sources — this compounds fast, since every accepted contract is a parallel stream of Funds/Science/Reputation.
  2. VAB/SPH next, once part-count limits start pinching. Early craft are usually small enough that the base limit is fine — upgrade when you actually find yourself unable to fit the parts a design needs.
  3. Tracking Station when you start flying multi-vessel or long-range missions. Extra tracking range and maneuver node counts matter much more once you're juggling satellites, relays, or interplanetary transfers than during early suborbital contracts.
  4. R&D and Astronaut Complex as science and crew needs grow. Push these when you're regularly capped on Science throughput or need more active Kerbals than your roster supports.
  5. Launch Pad/Runway only when a specific design needs it. These are often the last priority, since many effective early designs stay under the base mass/size limits by choice.

Strategies via the Administration building

Strategies let you trade one currency for another at a configurable rate, active continuously once selected (rather than a one-time transaction). Typical examples include converting a portion of incoming Science into Funds, or boosting the Funds reward from contracts at the cost of some Reputation.

When a trade-off strategy makes sense

Strategies are worth activating when you have a surplus of one resource and a shortage of another — for example, converting some Science into Funds early on if you're doing lots of low-risk orbital science but are cash-strapped for upgrades, then swapping back once Funds catch up and Science tech tree progress becomes the bottleneck instead. Because most strategies apply an ongoing rate rather than a one-time effect, revisit your active strategies periodically as your bottleneck shifts.

Recovering vessels and stages

Recovering a vessel — landing or splashing down within recovery range, or specifically recovering spent stages — returns a portion of that hardware's value as Funds. This makes reusability a real economic strategy in Career mode, not just an efficiency flex:

  • Recovering boosters (parachuted or powered-landing first stages) after they separate returns meaningful Funds compared to letting them fall into the ocean or crash uncontrolled.
  • Powered landings for an entire first stage (propulsive descent and landing, mirroring more advanced real-world reusable rocket designs) recover even more of the stage's value, at the cost of the fuel spent landing it.
  • Spaceplanes that land back on the runway under their own aerodynamic control recover close to the vehicle's full value with no extra fuel spent on a landing burn, which is part of why spaceplane-based launch systems are popular in Funds-constrained careers.

Over a full career save, consistent recovery practices meaningfully reduce your net cost per launch, freeing up Funds for facility upgrades and more ambitious contracts sooner.

A suggested early-career mission loop

New players often try to rush toward an ambitious first contract (an early Mun landing, say) before the Funds and Science base is in place to support it. A steadier loop looks like this:

  1. Fly cheap suborbital and orbital contracts first. These typically have low risk and low cost, and pay reasonably well in early-game terms relative to what they cost to fly.
  2. Farm easy Kerbin-system science alongside those contracts. Running the full set of stock experiments across Kerbin's biomes (and a first Mun/Minmus flyby or landing) generates a large amount of early Science cheaply, since these experiments haven't been "used up" yet.
  3. Reinvest Science into unlocking cheaper, more efficient parts — better engines and lighter structural parts reduce the cost of every future launch, which compounds.
  4. Use the resulting Funds surplus to upgrade Mission Control and the VAB/SPH so you can run more contracts in parallel and build larger craft.
  5. Only then move toward interplanetary contracts (Duna flybys/landings and beyond), once your Funds, Science, and facility levels can comfortably support the much higher cost and risk of those missions.
Don't front-load risk before you can absorb a failure

An expensive interplanetary mission failure early in a career — before your Funds and Reputation cushion is built up — can set a save back substantially further than the same failure would later. Build the base of Funds, Science, and Reputation first; treat riskier, higher-payoff missions as something you graduate into.

Difficulty settings for beginners

It's fine to soften some settings while learning

Career mode's difficulty options let you adjust several mechanics independently — you don't have to accept a preset difficulty wholesale. A few worth knowing about specifically as a new player:

  • Kerbal respawn: with respawn on, a Kerbal who dies becomes "missing" and eventually becomes available to recruit again instead of being gone permanently. Many beginners keep this on while learning landing and re-entry technique, since early mistakes are common and permanent Kerbal loss can feel unnecessarily punishing before you've built up piloting skill.
  • Re-entry heating: can be reduced or disabled entirely, which removes the risk of burning up during atmospheric entry while you're still learning periapsis targeting and craft orientation.
  • Resource transfer: some difficulty presets restrict free-form fuel transfer between tanks/vessels — an easier setting simplifies early ISRU and docking-refuel workflows while you're still learning them.

None of these settings need to stay soft forever — many players tighten them back up once they're comfortable with the underlying skills. There's no penalty for starting gentler.
