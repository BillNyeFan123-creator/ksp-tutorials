Anatomy of a basic rocket

A simple orbital rocket, built bottom to top in the VAB, usually looks like this:

  1. Engine

    Converts propellant into thrust. Bigger isn't automatically better — see thrust-to-weight ratio below.

  2. Fuel tank(s)

    Liquid fuel + oxidizer for most engines. Sized to give the stage enough delta-v for its job, not just "as much as fits."

  3. Decoupler

    Separates a spent stage from the rest of the rocket once its fuel is gone, so you stop hauling dead mass.

  4. (Repeat engine / tank / decoupler for each additional stage)

    Most orbital rockets to Kerbin orbit use two to three stages.

  5. Command pod

    Houses your Kerbal(s) and provides the "brain" needed to control the vessel. This is typically your root part.

  6. Parachute

    Mounted on or just above the pod for a safe return to Kerbin's surface.

  7. Fairing (optional but common)

    Protects a payload — a satellite, a lander, an antenna — from air resistance and heat during ascent. Covered fully in VAB Advanced Techniques.

Staging in depth

The staging list running down the right edge of the VAB screen controls exactly what happens, and in what order, every time you press Space in flight. KSP auto-groups parts into stages as you build, but the auto-grouping is not always what you want — always review it before you fly.

Staging rules of thumb
  • An engine and the decoupler directly below it are usually placed in the same stage so they fire together — ignition and separation happen in one keypress. You can also split them across two stages (ignite, wait, then decouple) for a controlled "ignite before drop" sequence with liquid engines, which avoids a brief thrust gap.
  • Group all parachutes into their own stage, positioned late enough that you won't accidentally trigger them while still under thrust or at high speed — deploying a chute above roughly 250-300 m/s tends to rip it off.
  • Solid rocket boosters (SRBs) cannot be throttled or shut off once lit. Plan their burn time into your staging order and don't stage past them before they've finished.
  • Use the small padlock/link icons between staging icons to merge multiple actions (e.g. four radial SRBs decoupling together) into a single stage that fires simultaneously.
  • Drag icons up or down the list to reorder stages if the auto-grouping put something in the wrong place — this is common with radially-attached boosters and fairings.
A note on asparagus staging

Advanced players often use asparagus staging — cross-feeding fuel from outer tanks into inner ones via fuel ducts so outer boosters drop away empty while inner engines keep running on full tanks. It's a big efficiency win on large rockets, but it needs fuel ducts and careful symmetry work, so full coverage lives in VAB Advanced Techniques. For now, just know the term exists.

Thrust-to-weight ratio (TWR)

TWR compares the thrust your engines produce to how much your rocket weighs under local gravity. Conceptually it's just:

TWR = Total thrust ÷ (Total mass × local gravity)

If TWR is below 1, your engines physically cannot lift the rocket off the ground — gravity wins. Above 1, the rocket accelerates upward; the higher above 1, the more aggressively it climbs. "Local gravity" matters because the same rocket that struggles off Kerbin (9.81 m/s²) might leap off the Mun (1.63 m/s²) with the same engines.

SituationTarget TWRWhy
Liftoff from Kerbin1.2 – 1.6Enough to climb without wasting fuel fighting drag from going too fast too low
Upper/vacuum stages0.5 – 1.0+No air resistance to fight and no gravity losses to rush past, so lower TWR is fine and saves mass
Landing on any body> 1.0 (local g)You must be able to fully cancel your descent velocity — anything less and you can only slow down, not hover or ascend
Eve ascent specifically2.0+Eve's thick atmosphere and 1.7g surface gravity punish anything underpowered

The VAB's stats panel shows TWR per stage — always check the first stage's TWR, since that single number determines whether you leave the pad at all.

Delta-v budgeting

Every maneuver in KSP costs a fairly predictable amount of delta-v (Δv), and your rocket only has so much to spend. You don't need to run the rocket equation by hand — the VAB stats panel and mods like Kerbal Engineer Redux (see Tips & Mods) calculate it for you — but understanding what's happening under the hood helps you design better rockets.

What the rocket equation is actually doing

Delta-v depends on two things: how fast your engine throws propellant out the back (exhaust velocity, directly tied to Isp — specific impulse) and your mass ratio — your full liftoff mass divided by your mass once the tank is empty. A higher-Isp engine gets more Δv per unit of propellant mass. A bigger mass ratio (a bigger tank relative to the dry rocket) also gives more Δv — but that relationship is logarithmic, not linear.

The tyranny of the rocket equation

Because the mass-ratio term is logarithmic, each extra unit of fuel gives you less additional Δv than the last, since the tank holding that fuel also has its own dry mass you now have to lift. Doubling your fuel does not double your Δv. Past a certain point, adding more tank is a losing trade — you get more value from a lighter craft, a better engine, or an extra stage than from just piling on more fuel. This is why efficient rocket design favors staging (dropping dead tank mass) over one giant single-stage fuel tank.

Worked example: budgeting a Mun round trip

Add up each leg you'll actually fly, then build with margin:

  • Kerbin surface to LKO: ~3,400 m/s
  • LKO to Mun intercept: ~860 m/s
  • Mun orbit insertion: ~310 m/s
  • Landing on the Mun: ~580 m/s
  • Ascent back to Mun orbit: ~580 m/s
  • Return transfer + Kerbin reentry: ~860 m/s

Running total: roughly 6,590 m/s. Add a 10–20% margin for gravity losses, steering inefficiency, and correction burns, and you should design for something like 7,200–7,900 m/s of total Δv across all stages, budgeted so each stage covers its own leg (or splits neatly across two).

Choosing the right engine

Match the engine to the stage's job, not just to "biggest number." A few stock workhorses:

EngineVacuum IspThrustBest role
LV-T30 "Reliant"~300s215 kNSimple first/launch stage; no gimbal, so pair with fins
LV-T45 "Swivel"~320s200 kNLaunch stage needing gimbal control for steering
RE-I5 "Skipper"~320s650 kNMid-size launch stage for heavier rockets
RE-M3 "Mainsail"~310s1,400 kNHeavy-lift launch stage / booster core
"Vector"~315s1,000 kNHeavy-lift with full gimbal range — great control authority
LV-909 "Terrier"~350s60 kNEfficient vacuum/upper stage, small landers
RE-L10 "Poodle"~350s250 kNVacuum stage for heavier payloads and transfer stages
T-1 "Dart"~340s180 kNToroidal shape suits asparagus-staged cores
LV-N "Nerv"~800s60 kNNuclear interplanetary cruise stages — huge Isp, needs heat management, poor at sea level
IX-6315 "Dawn"~4,200s2 kNIon propulsion for very light, long-duration probes only
  • Launch stages want high thrust and decent Isp — Reliant, Swivel, Skipper, or Mainsail depending on rocket size. Use gimballing engines (Swivel, Mainsail, Vector) unless your fins alone provide enough control.
  • Vacuum/transfer stages want high Isp over raw thrust — Terrier or Poodle are the standard picks for interplanetary and orbital-insertion stages.
  • Landers need TWR above 1 for the target body specifically — a Terrier is overkill-efficient for the Mun or Minmus but a Poodle or multiple engines may be needed for heavier landers on Duna or Tylo.
  • Nuclear cruise stages (Nerv) shine on long interplanetary burns where their terrible thrust is offset by having plenty of coast time, and their huge Isp dramatically cuts fuel mass for the trip.

Aerodynamics and stability basics

Full aerodynamics coverage — lift, control surfaces, reentry, spaceplanes — lives in Aerodynamics & Flight. For a first rocket, four basics cover almost every case:

  • Keep it pointy and smooth. A nose cone up front and a tapered profile noticeably cuts drag.
  • Put fins at the bottom, near the engines. Fins push the center of drag behind the center of mass, which makes the rocket want to fly straight — the same reason an arrow has feathers at the back, not the front.
  • Avoid large flat surfaces sticking out sideways low on the rocket, and avoid asymmetric designs unless you've deliberately compensated for them.
  • Check the Center of Mass (CoM) and Center of Lift/Drag indicators — toggle buttons near the bottom of the VAB screen — before flying anything unusual. Drag behind mass is stable; drag ahead of mass is not.
If your rocket flips during ascent

This is almost always one of: too much speed too low in the atmosphere (throttle back below roughly 10km), not enough fin authority at the tail, or turning too sharply during the gravity turn. Slow your turn rate and add tail fins before anything else.

The gravity turn, step by step

You cannot fly straight up the whole way to orbit — reaching orbit means moving sideways fast enough that you keep missing the ground, not just climbing. The gravity turn is the standard, fuel-efficient technique for trading "straight up" for "sideways" gradually.

  1. Launch straight up until roughly 50–100 m/s, or about 1km altitude.
  2. Pitch over 5–10° toward the east and let the rocket begin following that angled path. Launching eastward gives you a free velocity boost from Kerbin's own rotation.
  3. Let the nose gradually fall toward the horizon as you climb, following the prograde marker on the navball rather than fighting it back toward vertical.
  4. By around 45km altitude you should be flying nearly horizontal, with your apoapsis (the highest point of your orbit) sitting around 70-80km, or your intended orbital altitude.
  5. Coast to apoapsis, then burn prograde (level with the horizon) to raise your periapsis above the atmosphere — that's what actually circularizes the orbit. See Orbital Mechanics for exactly how maneuver nodes work.

Common beginner mistakes

Watch out for these
  • Forgetting a probe core or pod — a rocket needs one of these to be controllable at all, and probe cores also need electric charge and (usually) line of sight to the sun or a relay for full control.
  • No parachute, or a parachute in the wrong stage — always check your staging order before flying, not after you've fallen out of the sky.
  • Turning too fast or too early during ascent, overloading your fins and causing a flip.
  • Not enough struts on wide payloads or side-mounted boosters, causing wobble that can shake a rocket apart under thrust — see VAB Advanced Techniques for struts and autostrut.
  • Running out of electric charge on probes or unmanned stages — add batteries and/or solar panels, especially on anything without a crewed pod's built-in reserves.
  • Building "just enough" delta-v with no margin for correction burns, docking, or an imperfect ascent — always pad your budget by 10-20%.
  • Igniting SRBs without a plan to jettison them — a spent, unlit SRB is just dead weight you're now dragging to orbit.
  • Mixing engine types on one fuel line in ways that starve one engine — check fuel flow priority if you're combining SRBs and liquid engines or asymmetric tanks.
