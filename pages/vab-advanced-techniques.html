The symmetry tool

Symmetry mirrors or radially repeats a part as you place it, keeping multi-engine and multi-booster designs both faster to build and actually balanced.

  • Radial symmetry (2x, 3x, 4x, 6x, ...) arranges copies evenly around the vessel's center — the standard mode for boosters, radial engines, landing legs, and radial fuel tanks. Match the symmetry count to your design: 4 SRBs around a core stage uses 4x; a tripod lander uses 3x.
  • Mirror symmetry reflects a part across a single plane instead of arranging it radially — this is what you want for spaceplane wings, so the left and right wings are true mirror images rather than rotated copies (which would put control surfaces on the wrong edges).
  • Cycle the symmetry count with the keyboard shortcut shown in the VAB toolbar, and check the small on-screen indicator so you don't accidentally place 6 boosters when you meant 4.
  • Symmetry applies at the moment you place or move a part — if you need to change an existing radial part, you often need to delete and replace the whole symmetrical set, or use the part's right-click menu on versions that support live symmetry edits.
Radial vs mirror at a glance

If the part should point the same direction all the way around a central axis (boosters, legs, RCS thrusters), use radial. If the part is a left/right pair that should be true reflections of each other (wings, canards, some landing gear), use mirror.

Fuel ducts and asparagus staging

Asparagus staging is the classic technique for squeezing much more delta-v out of a wide rocket by changing the order fuel is consumed in, not the total amount of fuel carried.

The core idea

Normally, every engine burns fuel from its own tank, so all your tanks drain at roughly the same rate and go empty around the same time — meaning you're dragging several nearly-empty (but not massless) tanks right up until they're jettisoned together. Asparagus staging uses fuel ducts to pull fuel from outer tanks into inner tanks first. The outer tank/engine pairs run dry and get dropped early — shedding their dead weight — while every remaining engine keeps burning from tanks that are still nearly full, right up until the next pair drains.

  1. Build symmetrical radial stacks around a central core.

    Each radial stack has its own tank and engine, attached with radial decouplers so it can be jettisoned once empty.

  2. Run a fuel duct from each outer tank to the next tank inward.

    The fuel duct is a small part placed between two tanks that forces fuel to flow from the source tank into the target tank instead of feeding its own engine locally.

  3. All engines ignite together at launch, but because of the ducts, the outermost tanks empty first even though every engine is burning the whole time.
  4. Stage off each empty outer pair (engine + tank + decoupler) as it runs dry — you'll typically see this happen automatically in sequence if the staging list is set up correctly, each jettison lightening the rocket for the remaining engines.
  5. The core stack burns last, now benefiting from having spent the entire ascent feeding off tanks it was about to discard anyway, arriving at orbit dramatically lighter than a simple non-cross-fed design of the same total fuel.

Simple worked example

Picture a core stage with four identical radial boosters around it, each boosters' tank ducted into the core tank. All five engines burn from launch. As each radial tank empties, that engine+tank+decoupler set drops off — four separate small jettisons — while the core engine keeps burning continuously, fed in part by fuel that started in the boosters. By the time only the core remains, it's carrying a nearly full tank of its own plus whatever it pulled in, with none of the boosters' dead tank mass left to haul around. The net effect is more usable delta-v from the same total propellant than staging the boosters off with their own fuel unused.

Subassemblies and merging craft

Once you've built something reusable — a lander can with legs and a probe core, a standard docking adapter stack, a reliable upper stage — save it as a subassembly instead of rebuilding it from scratch every time.

  • Select the root part of the cluster you want to save, then use the subassembly save option (in the VAB's part-clump menu) to store it as a named, reusable block.
  • Subassemblies appear in their own tab in the parts sidebar and can be dragged into any future craft, keeping proven designs consistent across missions.
  • Merging craft files lets you load an entirely separate saved vessel into your current project — useful for combining a previously-designed lander with a new transfer stage, or building a space station module that needs to dock with an existing one for reference.
  • After merging, you'll often need to reattach the merged assembly to your main craft, and you may need to fix its root part or renumber symmetry before it behaves correctly.

Fairings

A fairing is a protective shell that encloses a payload during ascent and jettisons once you're clear of the atmosphere.

  • Protecting payloads from drag — an exposed satellite, antenna array, or lander creates a lot of drag and asymmetric aerodynamic force. A fairing turns that whole cluster into one smooth, low-drag shape.
  • Protecting payloads from heat — ascent heating can damage delicate parts; a fairing shields them until it's jettisoned in the near-vacuum of the upper atmosphere.
  • Hiding ugly hardware — many players fair over docking ports, radial parts, or asymmetric antenna clusters purely to avoid the drag and instability they'd otherwise cause, even when heat isn't a major concern.
  • Interstage fairings are a specific use case: a short fairing section that encloses an upper-stage engine bell against the base of the payload/next stage, smoothing what would otherwise be an exposed engine sticking out mid-rocket, then jettisoning with (or just after) that stage separates.
  • Fairing size and shape are set interactively in the VAB — click the fairing base and drag the included handles to size it around your payload, adding extra panel sections if needed for taller payloads.

Struts and autostrut

Large or oddly-shaped rockets often wobble under thrust — a visible, whip-like flex in the vessel that wastes control authority and can shake parts apart or snap the craft in half.

  • Physics wobble happens because parts are connected by joints that have some flex, and long, heavy, or asymmetric stacks (wide side-boosters, tall thin rockets, heavy payloads on a single connection point) amplify that flex into visible shaking.
  • Struts are physical parts you place between two points on your rocket to add a rigid connection, resisting bending between sections that would otherwise flex independently. Common placement: between radial boosters and the core, and along tall stacks with a heavy payload on top.
  • Autostrut (right-click a part → Autostrut) rigidly links a part to another part in the vessel without needing a visible strut part, at zero mass or drag cost. Options usually include:
    • Grandparent part — links to the part two attachment steps up the tree; good general-purpose default for reducing wobble on stacks.
    • Heaviest part — links to whatever the single heaviest part in the vessel is, useful for anchoring lightweight extremities (antennas, small radial parts) to the core mass.
    • Root part — links directly to the vessel's root part, giving a very stable but sometimes overly rigid connection.
  • Autostrut connections break automatically when the linked part is staged away, so they won't keep a jettisoned booster phantom-attached to your ship.
When to reach for which

Use autostrut liberally on wobble-prone stacks since it's free — set to "Heaviest Part" as a good default for most builds. Reach for physical struts specifically for radial boosters and cross-bracing wide payloads, since visually you often want the strut placement to be deliberate and symmetrical.

Action groups

Action groups let you bind a single keypress (1 through 0, plus a general "custom" set on more complex craft) to toggle any number of parts at once.

  • Common bindings: solar panel deploy/retract, lights on/off, landing gear up/down, parachute arm, engine toggle, cargo bay open/close, science experiment run.
  • Assign action groups in the VAB via the Action Groups tab — select a part, then click the action you want (e.g. "Toggle Panels") and assign it to a numbered group.
  • Group multiple different part types under one key when they logically happen together — for example, binding "gear down" and "lights on" to the same key for a night landing sequence.
  • On complex craft with many deployables, action groups are the difference between a calm, practiced landing sequence and frantically right-clicking a dozen parts in the VAB's part menu during a critical descent.

Offset, rotate, and attachment modes

  • Offset tool moves a part freely along an axis, letting you fine-tune spacing — for example, recessing a part slightly into another for a cleaner look, or adjusting radial part spacing so decouplers clear surrounding hardware.
  • Rotate tool spins a part around an axis — useful for angling radial parts, fixing an upside-down part after mirror symmetry, or aiming RCS thrusters correctly.
  • Radial attachment sticks a part to the side of another part at whatever point you click; stack attachment connects parts end-to-end along the vessel's centerline through defined nodes. Some parts (fuel tanks, engines) support both; others are restricted to one or the other.
  • Part clipping — using the offset tool to intentionally overlap two parts' models — is common for saving space (recessing landing legs into a fuselage, nesting batteries inside a service bay) but has limits: clipping fuel tanks into each other to "cheat" more fuel into a small space is generally considered poor etiquette for shared craft files and can behave oddly with collision detection. Use it for cosmetic tidiness, not to bypass part volume constraints.

Robotic parts and EVA construction

Two more advanced building tools exist beyond the base VAB toolset:

  • Robotic parts (Breaking Ground DLC) — hinges, pistons, and rotors let you build moving mechanisms directly into a craft: deployable arms, rotating solar arrays, extending landing gear on custom rovers, or robotic sample-return arms. These parts move in real time during flight and can be bound to action groups or the dedicated robotics control panel.
  • EVA construction — a Kerbal on EVA (with the right skill or era-specific tools) can pick up, carry, and attach individual parts directly in orbit or on a surface, effectively letting you "weld" pieces onto an existing craft or build small structures without returning to the VAB. This is commonly used for assembling large space stations from separately-launched parts, or field-repairing a station missing a part.
Full depth is in the DLC Overview

Both robotic parts and EVA construction come from the Breaking Ground expansion and have enough depth (joint limits, torque, part-attachment rules, construction mode controls) to warrant their own coverage — see DLC Overview for the complete rundown.
