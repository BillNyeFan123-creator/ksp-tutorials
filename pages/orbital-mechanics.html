Apoapsis, periapsis, and why burns work the way they do

Every orbit around a body has two extreme points. Apoapsis (Ap) is the highest point of the orbit — the farthest you get from the body's center. Periapsis (Pe) is the lowest point — the closest you get. Between those two points your orbit is an ellipse (a circle is just an ellipse where Ap and Pe happen to be equal).

The single rule that unlocks almost all orbital maneuvering: a prograde burn (speeding up in your direction of travel) raises the orbit on the opposite side from where you burn. A retrograde burn (slowing down) lowers the orbit on the opposite side. Burn prograde at periapsis and your apoapsis rises. Burn prograde at apoapsis and your periapsis rises.

Why the opposite side?

Orbits conserve energy. Adding speed at one point adds kinetic energy to the whole orbit, which raises the orbit's highest point — but that extra height only "shows up" on the far side of the ellipse from where you added the energy, because the point where you burned is now moving faster than a circular orbit would need, and it swings back down to that same altitude every lap. Think of it like throwing a ball harder horizontally — it doesn't shoot straight up from your hand, it goes farther before coming back down. The place you're standing (where you burned) stays fixed as your periapsis; the "farther before coming down" becomes your new, higher apoapsis on the other side.

This is why you can't raise your whole orbit evenly with a single burn from one point — you always raise or lower the far side first, and need a second burn at that new point to fix the near side (that second burn is exactly what circularizing is, covered next).

Reaching orbit and circularizing

After a gravity turn (covered on the Building Rockets page), you should be on a suborbital trajectory with your apoapsis climbing toward your target altitude — typically 75-100km around Kerbin, high enough to clear atmospheric drag but not so high that circularizing costs excessive Δv.

  1. Coast to apoapsis with the engine off once your apoapsis reaches your target altitude. If it's still climbing quickly, throttle down or cut the engine early — apoapsis keeps rising slightly after you cut thrust if you're still pointed prograde with any upward vertical velocity.
  2. Time-warp to apoapsis using physics warp (up to 4x) or regular warp if you're still deep in atmosphere-free space, watching the navball's time-to-apoapsis readout.
  3. Point prograde (use the prograde marker on the navball, or SAS prograde hold) and burn to raise periapsis. Watch the periapsis number in the map view or flight info — stop the burn once periapsis is at or above your target altitude (roughly equal to your apoapsis for a circular orbit).
  4. Fine-tune with short taps of prograde/retrograde once Ap and Pe are close to equal. A "circular enough" orbit for most purposes is within a few hundred meters at LKO altitudes.
Don't let periapsis dip back into atmosphere

If you delay the circularization burn too long after apoapsis, your orbit continues around and periapsis (still down in the atmosphere from your ascent) comes back up fast, costing you speed to drag. Circularize right at or just before apoapsis, not after you've coasted past it.

Maneuver nodes in depth

Maneuver nodes let you plan a burn in advance, see exactly what it will do to your orbit, and get a precise Δv and timing readout — indispensable for anything beyond "just circularize."

  1. Placing a node — in map view, click anywhere on your orbit line to drop a node. A small gizmo with colored handles appears at that point.
  2. Dragging the handles — prograde/retrograde (blue, along your direction of travel) change your speed at that point, which is what you'll use most; normal /antinormal (purple, perpendicular to your orbital plane) tilt the orbit's inclination; radial in/out (usually near the node, pointing toward/away from the body) shift the orbit shape without changing speed along the direction of travel — used less often, mostly for fine rendezvous adjustments.
  3. Reading the results — as you drag, the projected new orbit (a lighter-colored path) updates live, along with a Δv total and a predicted new Ap/Pe shown in the node's info panel.
  4. Time-to-node countdown — the node shows a countdown to when you'll reach it. Warp up to that point (KSP automatically slows warp as you approach a node) so you arrive with time to get in position.
  5. The "half the burn time before the node" rule — the node readout shows total burn time for the maneuver. Because the node marks the middle of your intended change (not the start), begin burning half that duration before you reach the node, so the burn is centered on it. For a 20-second burn, start at T-10 seconds.
  6. Executing the burn — point at the maneuver marker (a small blue-and-white circled icon on the navball, distinct from prograde), throttle up at your calculated start time, and watch the remaining Δv counter on screen tick down to zero. Cut throttle immediately at zero — overshooting wastes propellant and requires a correction burn.
  7. SAS maneuver node hold — with SAS on, click the SAS mode selector (or press the relevant hotkey) and choose the maneuver node icon. SAS will then automatically orient the craft to face the maneuver marker, freeing you to focus on throttle and timing.
Nodes are edits, not commitments

You can drag a node's handles as many times as you like, add multiple nodes along your orbit to plan out a multi-step trip, or delete a node entirely (right-click it and choose delete, or drag it off the orbit line) if your plan changes. Nothing happens until you actually burn.

Orbital periods and altitude

An orbit's period is how long it takes to complete one full lap. The relationship is simple: higher orbits have longer periods. A low Kerbin orbit at 80km takes roughly half an hour; geostationary altitude (~2,863km above Kerbin's surface) takes exactly one Kerbin sidereal day (~6 hours), matching the planet's rotation so the satellite hangs over the same point on the ground. Full geostationary placement mechanics — matching period precisely and zeroing inclination — are covered on the Satellites & Probes page.

Hohmann transfers

A Hohmann transfer is the standard fuel-efficient way to move from one circular orbit to another (higher or lower) circular orbit, using exactly two burns. It works whether you're raising your orbit around the same body, or transferring between two different bodies (the interplanetary version, covered in full on the Interplanetary Travel page, is the same idea applied at solar-system scale).

  1. Burn 1 — prograde at your starting orbit to raise (or retrograde to lower) your apoapsis (or periapsis) until it touches the target orbit's altitude. This puts you on an elliptical transfer orbit.
  2. Coast along the transfer ellipse until you reach the point where it touches the target orbit.
  3. Burn 2 — prograde (raising) or retrograde (lowering) at that point to circularize into the new orbit, matching your speed to the target altitude's circular velocity.

A concrete in-system example: going from a low 80km Kerbin orbit out to meet the Mun's orbit (roughly 12,000km out) uses this exact pattern — a prograde burn at LKO (~860 m/s) raises your apoapsis to intercept the Mun's orbital altitude, timed so the Mun is actually there when you arrive (see the rendezvous timing discussion on the Docking & Rendezvous page). From there, capturing into a Mun orbit is effectively the "second burn," just performed relative to the Mun's gravity well instead of Kerbin's.

Inclination and plane changes

Your orbital inclination is the tilt of your orbital plane relative to a reference plane (usually the target body's equator, or another craft's orbital plane for rendezvous). Two orbits around the same body cross each other at exactly two points — the ascending node (AN), where you cross from below the reference plane to above it, and the descending node (DN), where you cross back down.

  • A plane-change burn applies pure normal or antinormal thrust to rotate your orbital plane. It's cheapest when done exactly at an ascending or descending node, because that's the only place your velocity vector is already in the plane you're rotating around — burning normal/antinormal there changes only your inclination, not your speed or altitude.
  • Plane changes are also cheaper the slower you're moving, which means doing them far from the body (near apoapsis of a high, eccentric orbit) rather than close in, since the same angular change in velocity costs less Δv at lower orbital speed.
  • Combine plane changes with another burn whenever possible — for example, blend a normal/antinormal component into your circularization burn or your transfer injection burn, rather than doing a separate dedicated plane-change maneuver. KSP's maneuver node gizmo lets you drag both prograde and normal handles on the same node, and the game computes the combined (shorter, cheaper) Δv vector automatically.
Fixing inclination late is expensive

A plane change done deep in a low, fast orbit close to a planet can cost hundreds of m/s for even a small angle. Whenever possible, correct your inclination during launch (time your launch for when the target's orbital plane passes over your launch site) or early in a high, slow part of your trajectory — never as an afterthought once you're already circular and low.

Eccentricity, inclination, and spheres of influence

Eccentricity = 0Perfect circle
0 < e < 1Ellipse
e ≥ 1Escape trajectory

Eccentricity is a number describing how stretched an orbit's ellipse is: 0 is a perfect circle, values approaching 1 are increasingly stretched ellipses, and 1 or above means you're on a parabolic or hyperbolic path that escapes the body entirely rather than orbiting it. Inclination is simply the tilt angle of your orbital plane, in degrees, relative to the reference plane (0° is an equatorial orbit; 90° is a polar orbit).

Sphere of influence (SOI)

KSP uses a simplified "patched conic" model rather than full N-body gravity: at any moment, your craft is influenced by exactly one body's gravity, whichever one's sphere of influence you're currently inside. Kerbin's SOI extends far enough to contain the Mun and Minmus's orbits; once you cross into the Mun's much smaller SOI, your trajectory switches to being calculated purely relative to the Mun. This is why interplanetary and Mun/Minmus transfers are planned as a series of clean "hops" between one body's SOI and the next, rather than one continuously curving path — each SOI crossing is a sharp handoff in the game's math, shown in map view as your orbit line changing color and shape the instant you cross the boundary.
