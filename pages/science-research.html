What science points are for

In Science and Career modes, most parts start locked. Science points are the currency that unlocks nodes in the tech tree, which in turn unlock the parts you need for bigger and more capable missions. Every experiment you run and successfully get home (by transmission or physical return) converts into science points, so early-game missions are less about glory and more about efficient point-farming to unlock the parts that make later missions possible.

Sandbox mode skips this entirely

If you're playing Sandbox, every part is already unlocked and science points don't exist — this whole system only matters in Science mode and Career mode.

Experiment types

Every science experiment in the game falls into one of a few categories, and which category matters a lot for how you plan a mission around it.

  • Automated instruments like the thermometer, barometer, seismometer, and gravioli detector can be run any number of times immediately, in any situation, with no reset needed — they just won't give you more science for repeating the exact same situation and biome twice.
  • Mystery Goo canisters and Materials Bays are one-shot per situation/biome until reset. A Kerbal on EVA can manually reset one so it can be run again elsewhere on the same trip; without a Kerbal, you need a fresh, unused canister for each new biome you want data from.
Bring extra Goo canisters on probe-only landers

If your lander has no Kerbal to reset instruments, launch with one Mystery Goo canister and one Materials Bay per biome you plan to visit, rather than relying on resets you can't perform.

Situations, biomes, and the multiplier system

The same experiment doesn't just give a flat amount of science once — it can give more total science every time you run it in a genuinely new combination of situation and biome.

  • Situations are broad states: landed, splashed down, flying low in atmosphere, flying high in atmosphere, in space low, and in space high. The altitude bands defining "low" vs. "high" vary per body.
  • Biomes are named surface (and sometimes atmospheric/orbital) regions unique to each body — Mun Highlands and Mun Midlands are different biomes, and "Landed at Mun Highlands" is tracked as a completely separate result from "Landed at Mun Midlands," even for the identical experiment on the identical part.
  • The multiplier is per unique combination. Running a thermometer reading "Landed at Mun Highlands" pays out once at full value; running the identical experiment again in the same situation and biome pays out little to nothing extra, because the game already has that result on file. Move to a new biome, or change situation (e.g. go from landed to flying low), and it counts as new again.
Diminishing returns are real and immediate

Don't expect a second identical experiment in the same spot to be worth running — check the experiment's tooltip in-flight; it tells you directly whether the result is "new" or would only be worth a small fraction (or nothing) because it's already been recorded.

Transmitting vs. returning science

Once an experiment has produced a result, you have up to three ways to turn it into actual science points:

  1. Transmit it home via antenna. This is instant and doesn't require getting the craft back, but most experiments only transmit at partial value — some transmit at a high percentage, some (notably surface samples) cannot be transmitted at all and must be physically returned, and a few specialized experiments exist that are transmission-only.
  2. Physically return the data to Kerbin (splash down or land with the experiment data still stored on board, then recover the vessel). This always banks full value, at the cost of the time and Δv needed to bring the craft home.
  3. Process it in a Mobile Processing Lab. A crewed lab can slowly convert stored experiment data into science points over time on-site, without returning anything, and can also boost the transmission efficiency of data sent through it — a good middle ground for a long-duration base or station that will never come home.

The tech tree

Spending science points unlocks individual nodes in the tech tree, and each node unlocks a specific set of parts. Nodes generally get more expensive and more specialized the further out you go, and many require an already-unlocked prerequisite node before they become available.

  1. Start with basic engines, fuel tanks, and stability parts — you need a rocket that can reliably reach orbit before anything else matters.
  2. Unlock a better probe core and antenna early if you plan to lean on uncrewed missions for cheap science — see Satellites & Probes for what to look for.
  3. Grab basic science instruments (thermometer, barometer, Goo canister) as soon as you can afford them — they pay for themselves almost immediately once flown to even one new biome.
  4. Prioritize parachutes and landing legs so you can start doing full landed missions on the Mun and Minmus, which unlock a huge number of new situation/biome combinations at once.
  5. Save toward a Mobile Processing Lab once you're running longer or multi-body missions, since it meaningfully improves the return on data you'd otherwise transmit at a loss.

An efficient science-farming pattern

The most efficient early-to-mid game science strategy is a single reusable lander that hops between several biomes on the same body, collecting the full instrument suite at each stop before coming home once.

Example: a 3-biome Mun hopper
  1. Design a lander with enough Δv for a landing, a hop to a second biome, a hop to a third, and a return to Kerbin (or just enough for the hops if you plan to transmit and abandon it). Carry a thermometer, barometer, gravioli detector, Mystery Goo canister, Materials Bay, and a Kerbal if you want crew reports and surface samples too.
  2. Land in the first biome (e.g. Mun Midlands) and run every experiment: crew report, EVA report, surface sample, and all instruments.
  3. Reset the one-use instruments on EVA, board back up, and do a short suborbital hop to a second biome (e.g. Mun Highlands).
  4. Repeat the full experiment suite in the new biome — every one of these results is "new" and pays out in full.
  5. Hop to a third biome if Δv allows, repeat again, then either return to Kerbin for full-value science or transmit what you can and leave the rest banked for a Processing Lab later.
Minmus is even cheaper than the Mun for this

Minmus's lower gravity and flat biome-rich terrain make multi-biome hopping noticeably cheaper in Δv than the Mun, making it a strong first target for exactly this pattern.

EVA reports and surface samples

Two of the best science-per-part-count experiments in the game require nothing but a Kerbal and no dedicated instrument part at all:

  • EVA report — taken the moment a Kerbal steps outside on EVA, in any situation. It's free (no part mass) and pays out for each new situation/biome just like any other experiment, including ones you might not think to check, like flying low or high in an atmosphere during an EVA (rare, but possible on some craft) or simply being in space near a body you haven't visited.
  • Surface sample — taken while on EVA and landed or splashed down, using the Kerbal's own suit, no part required. Typically one of the higher-value single experiments in the game, but usually can't be transmitted — plan on bringing it home.
  • Don't skip low/high orbit and flying situations even without landing — a flyby or orbital pass over a body you haven't visited yet can bank an EVA report, crew report, and instrument readings for "in space low" and "in space high" without ever touching the surface, which is often the cheapest science in the game per unit of Δv spent.

Planning routes, not just rockets

Don't over-invest a huge rocket into one low-value stop

A giant, expensive lander that visits a single biome and comes straight home wastes most of its potential — the marginal cost of adding one more biome hop to an already-built lander is usually far cheaper than the cost of the mission that got you there in the first place. Before launching, sketch out which 2-4 biomes you can realistically reach with the Δv on hand, and route the mission through all of them.

  • Prioritize new biomes and new situations over repeating ones — a repeat is worth little to nothing, while an unvisited biome or situation is worth full value even for an experiment you've run a hundred times elsewhere.
  • Check the in-flight tooltip before running an experiment — it tells you up front whether the result would be new (full value), diminished (partial, already partly recorded), or exhausted (nothing left to gain), so you're never guessing.
  • Think in terms of "science per launch," not "science per experiment" — a mission plan that squeezes five biomes out of one launch will always out-earn five separate single-biome launches for the same total Δv budget.

Once you've maximized what a single body can offer, the next step up is spreading this same biome-hopping discipline across other planets — see Interplanetary Travel for how to actually get a science lander to Duna and beyond.
