Why bring a rover

A lander gives you exactly one landing site and whatever biome it happens to touch down in. A rover turns that single site into a whole region: you can drive to multiple science biomes from one landing, reach specific points of interest like anomalies or interesting terrain features spotted from orbit, and cover long-range surface distances that would otherwise need a separate landing (and a separate ascent stage) for every location.

Rover design basics

  • Wheels have a maximum safe speed — push a wheel faster than its rated speed for too long and it overheats and can explode. Check each wheel part's stats and don't rely on full-throttle driving everywhere, especially downhill where gravity adds to your speed for free.
  • Low, wide wheelbase — a rover that's wider than it is tall, with wheels set as far out as practical, resists tipping far better than a tall, narrow design.
  • Keep the center of mass low — mount heavy parts (batteries, fuel, science instruments) as close to the ground/chassis as you can rather than stacking them up high.
  • Reaction wheels and RCS give you a way to fight a tip-over in progress or roll the rover back upright afterward — very cheap insurance for how often low-gravity rovers get airborne over bumps.
  • Lights for driving on the night side or into shadowed craters, since low-gravity moons like the Mun and Minmus have long nights (matching Kerbin's ~6 hour day/night split as seen from their orbit) and no atmosphere to scatter any ambient light.
  • Uncrewed rovers need a probe core, battery, and enough solar panel or RTG capacity to stay powered and in control the whole route, exactly like any other probe.

Low gravity means low traction

Wheels grip by pressing down into the ground, and weight (not mass) is what provides that downforce. On Minmus at roughly 1/20th of Kerbin gravity, your rover has far less traction than the same design would on Kerbin, making it much easier to spin wheels, slide, or become briefly airborne over even small bumps.

Attaching a rover to a lander

Most rovers travel to their destination attached to a lander, then detach once safely on the surface.

  1. Cradle or ramp design — mount the rover on a flat structural platform on the side or top of the lander, with the wheels resting against simple angled ramp pieces or a decoupler-based cradle, so that after decoupling, gravity (or a gentle nudge) lets it roll or drop cleanly onto the ground.
  2. Decouple and drive off — trigger the decoupler holding the rover, then carefully drive forward and off the cradle/ramp, watching for any remaining attachment points or clipping parts that could snag.
  3. Dedicated rover-carrying truss — for larger rovers, a purpose-built truss or bay slung underneath or beside the lander, released by a decoupler once landed, gives more clearance than a simple side-mount and avoids the rover's wheels touching the lander's own landing legs.
Test the release on the launch pad or in a fresh save

Rover cradle designs are notorious for parts clipping or the rover flipping the instant it decouples due to leftover velocity or an uneven ramp angle. Test the decouple-and-drive-off sequence before committing to the full mission.

Driving technique in low gravity

  1. Use gentle, partial throttle inputs rather than full acceleration or full braking — low gravity means wheels have less grip, so sudden inputs are far more likely to cause a spin or a bounce than the same input would on Kerbin.
  2. Slow down before cresting a hill — driving over a rise at speed launches the rover briefly airborne, and in low gravity "briefly airborne" can mean several seconds of freefall before you land hard, often at a bad angle.
  3. Brake early and gradually on descents, since low gravity also means less resistance slowing you down naturally, so speed builds up faster than intuition suggests.
  4. Use SAS for stability — SAS's reaction-wheel-driven leveling can help damp out the small rocks and tips that come from driving over rough terrain, especially at speed.
  5. Recovering a tipped-over rover — try, in order: (a) RCS translation/rotation thrust if the rover has RCS ports, gently rolling it back upright; (b) a Kerbal on EVA physically pushing or pulling the rover chassis (works surprisingly well in low gravity); (c) rocking the rover's own reaction wheels side to side to build up a rolling motion that tips it back over.
A hard flip can end the rover permanently

Flipping at high speed can break wheels, solar panels, or antennas off entirely, and a rover with no wheels left is stranded for good. Better to arrive slowly than to arrive fast and risk the whole vehicle.

Planning a surface science route

  1. Check the biome map before landing (available in map view or via a scanning satellite in orbit) so you know roughly how many distinct biomes are within driving range of your planned landing site.
  2. Land near a biome border when possible, so a short drive in either direction crosses into a new science biome rather than needing a long trek for each one.
  3. Run each experiment in every biome you visit — most stock experiments give fresh science per biome per situation (landed vs. splashed, etc.), so revisiting the same experiment across multiple biomes is one of the most efficient ways to rack up science points.
  4. Plan your route as a loop back toward your lander or a dedicated return vehicle, rather than a straight line out, so you're not stranded far from your ride home.
  5. Watch daylight and power — solar-powered rovers lose charging capacity at night; time long drives to finish before dark, or carry enough battery/RTG capacity to keep driving (and keep instruments and lights running) through a full night cycle if your route requires it.

Rovers vs. hopping landers

Wheels aren't always the right tool. An alternative is a small lander that "hops" — short suborbital flights using its own engine to relocate — rather than driving. Hopping works better than a rover for extremely rough or steep terrain where wheels would struggle or flip constantly, and for covering very large distances quickly, since a hop can cross terrain in seconds that would take a rover many minutes of careful driving. The tradeoff is Δv: every hop costs propellant, while a rover's wheels are essentially free to run (aside from any electric charge draw) once you're on the ground.

Power and instrument budgeting

A surface rover's power budget is easy to overlook until it strands you mid-route. Every science instrument draws a jolt of charge when activated, wheels and reaction wheels draw a steady trickle while driving, and lights draw continuously whenever on. Before committing to a long route:

  • Add up peak draw — wheels, lights, an active experiment, and any transmitting antenna running at once — against your battery capacity and solar/RTG regeneration rate.
  • Plan around the local day/night cycle. The Mun and Minmus share Kerbin's roughly 6-hour day length as seen from orbit, but a rover parked in a crater's shadow can lose solar power long before actual local "night" if terrain blocks the sun angle.
  • RTGs remove the guesswork for uncrewed rovers on long routes, at the cost of extra mass and part cost, since they generate a small constant trickle regardless of sunlight or orientation.

Recovery tools and CommNet

Remote rovers still need CommNet coverage

An uncrewed, probe-core-controlled rover follows the exact same CommNet rules as any other probe — it needs a signal path back to Kerbin, directly or through a relay, to be fully controllable. Losing signal (for example, driving into a crater or behind a ridge that blocks line of sight) can leave it stuck with no steering input until the connection is restored. See the Satellites & Probes page for full CommNet mechanics.

Optional recovery mods

Several community mods add tools for recovering flipped rovers or stabilizing rough terrain driving. They're entirely optional — everything above works in stock KSP — and the full recommended mod list lives on the Tips & Mods page.
