Landing on an airless body

On a body with no atmosphere — the Mun, Minmus, Ike, Gilly, Tylo, Vall — your engine is the only thing that slows you down. There is no drag to help, so every m/s of descent speed you carry near the surface has to be cancelled with thrust. That makes airless landings a pure fuel and timing problem, and it's where the "suicide burn" technique pays off most.

The suicide burn

A suicide burn (also called a "deorbit burn to landing" burn) means waiting as long as possible before firing your engine retrograde, so that your velocity reaches zero at almost the exact instant you touch the surface. It's called "suicide" because if you're even a few seconds late, you hit the ground while still falling fast — there's no margin for error once you commit.

  1. Lower your periapsis into the terrain. From a low, roughly circular orbit, burn retrograde until your periapsis is at or just below the surface near your intended landing site — this aims your trajectory at the ground instead of just skimming past it.
  2. Watch your radar altitude and surface speed. Radar altitude (surface altitude) is what matters here, not the sea-level altimeter reading — mountainous terrain can be hundreds of meters "taller" than the reference sea level, and burning based on the wrong number is a common way to slam into a hillside.
  3. Calculate (or eyeball) your burn start point. A rough rule of thumb: the altitude at which you should start burning full throttle is approximately where your remaining fall time, multiplied by your deceleration rate, would bring your velocity to zero right at the surface. Many players just watch their surface speed vs. altitude ratio and start burning when those two numbers are getting close (e.g. 150 m/s at 1500m altitude with a craft that decelerates around 15-20 m/s² is roughly the right ballpark to start).
  4. Burn hard, then throttle down as you slow. Start at or near full throttle, then ease off as your vertical speed approaches zero, aiming to hit roughly 0 m/s at just a few meters above the ground.
  5. Kill any remaining horizontal velocity before you touch down — a lander with sideways motion at touchdown is far more likely to tip over, even at low speeds.
Tip: use the retrograde marker

Keep the retrograde marker centered on your navball throughout the burn. That guarantees your thrust is working directly against your current motion, which is the most fuel-efficient way to cancel velocity — any burn that isn't purely retrograde wastes propellant fighting a component of motion you didn't need to cancel.

The safer alternative: hover descent

If you're not confident in your timing yet, a hover descent trades fuel for safety. Instead of one committed burn, you kill your horizontal velocity early and then descend slowly under continuous partial thrust, adjusting throttle to control your rate of fall the whole way down.

Suicide burn vs. hover descent
  • Suicide burn: most fuel-efficient, but unforgiving — a late start means a crash, an early start just costs a bit of extra fuel hovering while you correct.
  • Hover descent: costs noticeably more Δv (you're fighting gravity for a longer time instead of free-falling most of the way down), but gives you constant control and time to react to terrain, so it's much harder to crash while learning.

A good middle ground once you're comfortable: start with a rough suicide burn to kill most of your speed, then finish the last 100-200m as a slow, controlled hover descent.

Landing with an atmosphere

An atmosphere does a lot of the braking work for you for free, which saves a large amount of Δv compared to an airless landing — but it adds several new steps, and getting them in the wrong order is one of the most common ways new players lose a Duna or Eve lander.

  1. Target your periapsis into the atmosphere. Rather than fully capturing into a stable orbit first, you can often save fuel by aiming your incoming periapsis directly into the upper atmosphere on your very first pass — this uses drag instead of a capture burn to start shedding your interplanetary approach velocity. If you want more control over your landing site and timing, capture into a low orbit first instead, then plan your deorbit from there.
  2. Fly heat-shield-first. Orient your craft so a heat shield (or the most heat-resistant part of your ship, like a capsule's ablative shield) faces directly into the direction of travel. Coming in sideways or engine-first exposes parts that aren't rated for re-entry heat and can explode long before you reach the ground.
  3. Let the atmosphere do the work, then deploy parachutes. Once you've slowed enough and dropped low enough, deploy parachutes. Parachutes need sufficient air density to generate drag, so the altitude at which they become effective depends entirely on the destination.
  4. Finish with a powered landing burn if needed. If your descent speed under parachutes alone is still too fast for the terrain (very common on Duna, where chutes alone rarely get you below ~7-10 m/s), fire your engine for the last few hundred meters to bring your final touchdown speed down further.
Thin atmospheres need lower parachute deployment

Duna's atmosphere is only about 6-7% as dense as Kerbin's at sea level, so parachutes there are far less effective at any given altitude. Where a Kerbin re-entry can fully deploy chutes comfortably above 1000m, a Duna landing usually needs chutes set to deploy much lower (often under 500m, and even then only partially slowing you) — plan on combining chutes with a powered landing burn on Duna almost every time, rather than treating chutes as sufficient on their own.

Eve: bring way more engine than you think

Eve's atmosphere is thick and its surface gravity is high, so while parachutes work well there for slowing down, getting back off the surface afterward needs an enormous amount of Δv relative to any other body in the game. If you're not specifically planning an Eve return, treat an Eve landing as one-way.

Landing legs and craft balance

A lander that runs out of fuel gracefully but tips over on touchdown still counts as a failed mission for anything you needed to launch again from the surface. Balance and leg placement deserve as much attention as the descent itself.

  • Place legs wider than your center-of-mass profile. A tall, narrow stance — common on landers built by stacking parts straight up — tips over easily on even a gentle slope or slight sideways touchdown speed. Angle legs outward from the body, or mount them on extended structural elements, so your footprint is noticeably wider than your craft is tall.
  • Deploy legs before you touch down, not after. Legs are usually toggled from the right-click part menu or a bound action group — extend them well before final approach; a craft that lands on its engine bell or a tank instead of its legs will almost always tip or explode.
  • Keep your center of mass low. Heavy tanks or engines mounted high up raise your center of mass and make tipping more likely even with wide legs. Where possible, put heavier components toward the bottom of the stack.
  • On sloped terrain, prioritize a level touchdown over a precise landing spot. A perfectly targeted landing site on a 20° slope is far riskier than a slightly-off landing on flat ground. If you can see the terrain isn't level as you descend, it's usually worth translating sideways during the final approach to find flatter ground, even at the cost of a bit of extra Δv.
  • Touch down with minimal horizontal velocity. Vertical speed alone is fairly forgiving (a few m/s is typically fine for stock legs), but horizontal "skidding" speed at contact is what most often causes a tip-over, since it applies sideways torque against the legs.

Activities on the surface

Once you're down safely, a landed craft opens up several options: run science experiments (right-click instrument parts, or send a Kerbal out on EVA for a surface sample and crew report), plant a flag, and — if you brought one — drive a rover out to reach additional science biomes without needing to relaunch and re-land. These activities are covered in much more depth on their own pages: see Science & Research for experiment strategy and the Mobile Processing Lab, and Rovers & Surface Ops for driving technique and multi-biome routes.

Ascent and the return transfer

Getting back off a body works the same way launching from Kerbin does, scaled to local conditions: point mostly upward initially, perform a gravity turn toward orbital velocity if the body has meaningful gravity, and burn to a stable low orbit. On an airless body with low gravity (Minmus, Gilly) this is a short, cheap burn; on a body with an atmosphere (Duna) you'll want a shallower ascent profile similar to a Kerbin launch to avoid excess drag losses.

Once in a stable orbit, plan your return transfer the same way you planned the trip out: for the Mun or Minmus, a straightforward prograde burn timed to intersect Kerbin's sphere of influence is usually enough; for another planet, you're planning a full interplanetary transfer window back toward Kerbin — see Interplanetary Travel and Advanced Maneuvers for the ejection-angle and transfer-window techniques that apply equally well in reverse.

Budgeting your return fuel

Budget your return fuel before you land — not after

The single most common way to strand a crew is spending too much Δv on the descent and on surface exploration, then discovering there isn't enough fuel left for ascent plus the transfer home. Always check your remaining Δv against that cost before committing to extra surface activities — once you've landed, it's often too late to change your mind.

Worked example — a Mun lander: suppose your lander has 1200 m/s of Δv remaining after a suicide-burn landing. Ascent to a stable low Mun orbit costs roughly 580 m/s, and the return transfer plus a Kerbin re-entry-capture margin costs another roughly 300 m/s (the atmosphere handles the rest of the deceleration at Kerbin, so you don't need a full capture burn). That's about 880 m/s committed to just getting home, leaving roughly 320 m/s of margin. If you're tempted to hop to a second biome for more science, check the hop's estimated cost against that 320 m/s margin first — don't discover the shortfall after you've already burned the fuel.

Leave a personal safety margin

Don't plan down to the last few m/s. Engine efficiency, imprecise burns, and an extra correction burn or two all eat into your margin — treat your calculated "safe" leftover Δv as a ceiling, not a target, and leave at least 10-15% of it unspent as a buffer.

Kerbin re-entry, step by step

  1. Lower your periapsis to roughly 30-45km. Deep enough to reliably catch atmosphere and bleed off your velocity, but not so deep that you generate excessive, unnecessary heat. Shallower periapsis values (closer to 45km) give a longer, gentler reentry; deeper values (closer to 30km) are more aggressive and heat your craft faster.
  2. Orient heat-shield-first. Point retrograde with your ablative heat shield (or your capsule's heat-shield-rated base) facing directly into the direction of travel, and hold that attitude through peak heating. Tumbling or facing the wrong way during reentry is one of the most reliable ways to lose a capsule that would otherwise have survived easily.
  3. Ride it out. Peak heating happens early in the descent as you're still moving fast through progressively thicker air — resist the urge to do anything except hold your attitude until your velocity has dropped substantially and the heat effects fade.
  4. Deploy parachutes once below roughly 1-2km altitude and slow enough for safe deployment — stock chutes will typically show "semi-deployed" (a small drag-inducing pre-deploy state) until conditions are safe, then fully deploy automatically.
  5. Touch down or splash down. Both are perfectly fine outcomes for a parachuted capsule — Kerbin is mostly ocean, so plan on the possibility of a splashdown and make sure your capsule floats or that recovery doesn't depend on a land touchdown specifically.
Recovering the vessel

Once safely down (or splashed down) and no longer moving, use the Recover Vessel option from the tracking station or flight UI to bank any Funds and Science the mission earned — see Career Mode for how recovery ties into your overall Funds economy.
