How stock aerodynamics actually works

Since the "FAR-lite" aero overhaul, stock KSP models two separate forces on every part: drag and lift. You don't need the underlying math, but the shape of the model matters for design decisions.

  • Drag depends mainly on a part's cross-sectional shape and orientation relative to the airflow, and on how dense the air is. Blunt, wide, sideways-facing shapes generate far more drag than smooth, tapered, nose-first shapes.
  • Air density falls off with altitude. Kerbin's atmosphere effectively ends around 70km; drag is strongest near sea level and fades to nothing by the time you're above roughly 35-40km, which is why ascent profiles push toward horizontal well before that altitude.
  • Lift comes from wings and control surfaces (and, to a smaller extent, from body-lift on some capsule shapes) when they meet the airflow at an angle of attack — the angle between the wing and the direction of travel. Zero angle of attack on a symmetric wing produces no lift; tilt it slightly and lift increases, up to a point where it stalls.
  • Intakes and engines care about airflow, not just altitude — air-breathing jet engines need sufficient intake air, which thins with altitude and eventually forces a transition to rocket power on any spaceplane heading to orbit.
Why this matters for design

Every part contributes both mass and drag. A rocket with excess radial fuel tanks, exposed ladders, or an un-faired payload is paying a drag tax the whole way up. Smooth, symmetric, tapered designs consistently out-perform lumpy ones for the same amount of fuel.

Center of mass, center of lift, center of drag

The VAB shows three toggleable indicators near the bottom of the screen: CoM (center of mass, a yellow/blue ball), CoL (center of lift, a green symbol shown when airflow is enabled), and effectively a center of drag, which isn't shown directly but can be reasoned about from where your draggy parts sit.

The arrow-and-feather analogy

Think of your rocket like an arrow. An arrow flies straight because its feathers (fletching) sit behind the arrow's center of mass — if the wind pushes the tail sideways, the extra drag/lift at the back rotates the arrow back into line with its direction of travel. Flip the fletching to the front and the arrow tumbles uncontrollably. A rocket with fins at the tail and a nose cone up front behaves the same way: drag/lift behind the center of mass is stable, and drag/lift ahead of the center of mass is unstable and will actively try to flip you.

  • For a rocket, aim to keep the center of drag (roughly: where your fins and any draggy radial parts are) clearly behind the center of mass.
  • For a spaceplane, the relevant relationship is between CoM and CoL: keep the CoL slightly behind the CoM for stable, controllable flight. CoL noticeably ahead of CoM makes the plane want to pitch up uncontrollably (and often flip); CoL far behind CoM makes it sluggish and hard to rotate.
  • Both indicators shift as you burn fuel — a design that's stable full may become nose- or tail-heavy once tanks empty. Check CoM position at both full and (roughly) empty fuel states for anything unusual.

Why rockets flip during ascent

A "flip" is when aerodynamic forces overpower your control authority and the rocket snaps sideways or end-over-end. It has a small number of common causes:

CauseFix
Going too fast, too low in the atmosphere (high dynamic pressure)Throttle back below roughly 10-12km until you're higher and the air is thinner
Turning too hard during the gravity turnPitch over gradually — a few degrees at a time, not a sharp 45° snap
Insufficient fin authority at the tailAdd or enlarge fins near the engines; consider canards only if you know what you're doing
Center of drag ahead of center of massMove draggy parts (radial boosters, exposed tanks) toward the back, or add a nose cone to clean up the front
Asymmetric build (uneven radial parts, offset parts)Use symmetry mode consistently; check the VAB's CoM indicator for obvious offset
The classic beginner flip

Full throttle straight off the pad, then a hard turn at 15km while still doing 400+ m/s, is the single most common way new players lose a rocket. Fix it by throttling down through max dynamic pressure (usually noted as "Max Q," roughly 8-12km on a typical ascent) and by turning gradually instead of all at once.

Reentry heating basics

Coming back from orbit means shedding a huge amount of velocity, and in stock KSP that energy shows up as heat on any part exposed to the airflow.

  • Heat shields (ablative shields, or a capsule's own built-in ablator on some pods) absorb heat by slowly burning away a consumable "ablator" resource. Once ablator runs out, the part behind it starts taking full heat damage.
  • Fly blunt-end-first. A wide, flat heat shield facing directly into the airflow spreads heat over a larger area and creates a shockwave that stands off from the vessel, which is dramatically safer than a pointy or sideways reentry.
  • Keep your reentry angle moderate. Too shallow and you can skip off the atmosphere or spend a long time overheating at high speed; too steep and you convert velocity to heat very quickly over a short time, spiking part temperatures. A periapsis of roughly 30-45km on a return from LKO is a reasonable starting target for stock capsules.
  • Check part temperature bars that appear during a hot reentry — a part flashing or glowing with a temperature gauge climbing toward its max is at risk of exploding if you don't shed speed or protect it.
  • Interplanetary returns (from Duna, Eve, Jool's system, etc.) come in much faster than a simple Mun return and need a more generous heat shield margin and often an aerobraking pass at a higher periapsis first.
Don't reenter sideways

A capsule or spaceplane that tumbles during reentry exposes unshielded parts — antennas, solar panels, docking ports — to direct heating, which can destroy them (and eventually the craft) even if your main heat shield is fine. Keep SAS on retrograde hold (see below) through the hottest part of reentry.

Introduction to spaceplane aerodynamics

Spaceplanes fly by generating lift from wings instead of relying purely on thrust, which changes the whole flight technique compared to a rocket.

  • Wings provide the lift needed to get airborne and to control descent gracefully. More wing area means more lift at a given speed, but also more drag and mass.
  • Control surfaces — elevons (pitch and/or roll), ailerons (roll), and a rudder (yaw) — are usually small moving sections built into or attached near the wings and tail. Assign them correctly (pitch/roll/yaw enabled per surface) or you'll find the plane hard to control.
  • Takeoff technique differs from a rocket launch. A spaceplane needs enough runway speed for its wings to generate lift equal to its weight before rotating (pitching up) — this is usually a fast, shallow acceleration down the runway rather than a vertical climb, and pulling up too early at too low a speed just drags the tail and wastes runway.
  • Angle of attack management is constant work in a spaceplane: too little and you're not generating enough lift or climbing fast enough; too much and you stall (airflow separates from the wing and lift collapses) or generate so much drag you can't accelerate through the transonic/supersonic region.
  • Respect your parts' limits. Wings and intakes have maximum airspeed/heat tolerances before they rip off or overheat — pushing a subsonic-rated wing design well past its intended speed, or starving jet engines of intake air at altitude, are both common ways a spaceplane mission ends early. Watch part stress indicators during any high-speed, low-altitude flight.
A practical spaceplane climb profile

Accelerate level or in a shallow climb until comfortably supersonic, then pitch up gradually toward a steeper climb as air thins and jet thrust weakens, switching to rocket engines once intake air can no longer sustain your jets — typically somewhere around 18-25km depending on your intake count and engine choice.

SAS modes and manual trim

SAS (Stability Assist System) does more than just "hold still." Once unlocked (via a pilot on board or an advanced probe core), cycling through SAS modes gives you several hold behaviors:

SAS modeWhat it holds
Stability AssistYour current orientation, resisting any drift — the default mode
Prograde / RetrogradePoints along, or directly against, your current velocity vector
Normal / Anti-normalPoints perpendicular to your orbital plane — used for plane-change burns
Radial in / Radial outPoints toward or away from the body you're orbiting — used for some orbit-shaping burns
Target / Anti-targetPoints toward or directly away from a selected target vessel — useful for docking approaches
Maneuver nodePoints along the direction of your next planned maneuver node burn

SAS is a stabilizer, not an autopilot — it holds a direction but won't fly a full ascent or landing for you. On aircraft, SAS can also fight your manual inputs slightly; many spaceplane pilots use trim (typically Alt + the pitch/yaw/roll keys) to set a small constant control input — e.g. slight up-trim to hold level flight hands-off — without SAS constantly fighting your stick input.

Reading failure and stress indicators

What the game is trying to tell you
  • A part temperature gauge appears and climbs during high-speed atmospheric flight or reentry — a bar filling toward red means that part is approaching structural or thermal failure.
  • Aerodynamic stress/G-force warnings can appear on parts under extreme dynamic pressure or acceleration, particularly wings and fins pushed well past a sane airspeed at low altitude.
  • Parts flash or glow before they explode from overheating — this is your warning window to reduce speed, adjust attitude, or (if reentering) hope your heat shield still has ablator left.
  • A sudden violent rotation with no input from you almost always means you've crossed into an aerodynamically unstable flight regime — cut throttle and try to point retrograde immediately rather than fighting it with raw control input.
