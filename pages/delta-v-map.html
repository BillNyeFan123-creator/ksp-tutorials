How to read a delta-v map

A delta-v map breaks a mission into individual "legs" — one maneuver each, like "low Kerbin orbit to Mun encounter" or "Duna orbit to Duna surface." Add the legs of your planned route together to get a rough total Δv budget for the whole mission, then size your rocket's stages to that total plus margin.

Most published delta-v maps (including this one) show numbers as a branching tree radiating outward from Kerbin's surface: a short trunk of cheap, shared legs (surface to LKO, LKO to Kerbin escape) that then splits into separate branches per destination. You don't need every branch — just add up the specific chain of legs your mission actually flies, from the trunk out to your destination and, if applicable, back again.

A useful habit: write your planned leg sequence down as a plain list before you open the VAB. For a Duna round trip, that list looks like "surface→LKO, LKO→escape, escape→Duna transfer, Duna aerobrake capture, Duna orbit→landing, Duna landing→orbit, Duna→Kerbin transfer, Kerbin reentry." Summing the table values for exactly that list is far less error-prone than trying to estimate a whole mission's cost in your head.

One-way vs. round trip

Numbers below are one-way legs. A round-trip mission needs the outbound legs and the return legs (ascent from a body, transfer home, and reentry/capture at Kerbin) — don't forget to double-count the trip home when budgeting a sample-return or crewed mission.

It also helps to separate your budget into stages that will actually be separate hardware stages on your rocket. A "Kerbin surface to LKO" leg is almost always your first-stage/second-stage combo and gets dropped before you ever leave Kerbin's atmosphere; a "Duna landing" leg is a dedicated lander that might get left behind on the surface entirely. Matching Δv legs to physical stages up front avoids the classic mistake of building one enormous single-stage craft sized to a mission total that no single stage actually needs to carry alone.

Notice how much cheaper Minmus's landing leg is than the Mun's, despite Minmus being farther away — its much lower gravity more than makes up for the longer trip. See the Planet-by-Planet Guide for why. This is also why a Minmus mission is often cheaper end-to-end than a Mun mission even though the Mun encounter leg itself is slightly cheaper — the landing and ascent legs at Minmus are so much lower that they more than make up the difference over a full round trip.

Eve ascent is the single most expensive maneuver in stock KSP

Launching off Eve's surface back to orbit can cost 8,000 m/s or more depending on your ascent profile and landing altitude. No other maneuver in the stock game comes close — budget for it as its own dedicated mission, not a line item in a bigger one. See the Eve entry in the Planet-by-Planet Guide for ascent design tips.

Jool-moon-specific numbers (Laythe, Vall, Tylo, Bop, Pol) vary a lot moon to moon and are covered in detail on the Planet-by-Planet Guide rather than duplicated here.

Special case: Tylo

Tylo landing~2,800 m/s
Tylo ascent~2,800+ m/s
WhyKerbin-level gravity, zero atmosphere

Tylo deserves its own callout because it breaks the usual pattern: airless bodies are normally cheap to land on and leave. Tylo has Kerbin-comparable surface gravity with no atmosphere to help either direction, so both landing and ascent cost roughly as much as a full Kerbin launch — each way. Treat a Tylo leg as two separate Kerbin-launch-sized budget items, not a typical moon landing.

Worked mission budgets

Adding legs together turns the abstract table above into an actual number you can design a rocket around. A few common mission shapes, totaled from the legs above:

MissionLegs summedApprox. total (m/s)
Kerbin → Mun landing → return Surface-to-LKO + Mun encounter + Mun orbit insertion + Mun landing + Mun surface-to-reentry ~6,190
Kerbin → Minmus landing → return Surface-to-LKO + Minmus encounter + Minmus landing + return leg (comparable to outbound) ~5,900
Kerbin → Duna landing (one-way probe) Surface-to-LKO + Kerbin escape + Duna transfer + Duna landing (aerobrake-assisted capture) ~5,860
Kerbin → Duna landing → return Above, plus Duna surface-to-orbit + Duna-to-Kerbin transfer + Kerbin reentry ~9,000+
Kerbin → Tylo landing → return Surface-to-LKO + Kerbin escape + Jool transfer + Jool-system transfer to Tylo + Tylo landing + Tylo ascent ~12,000+

These totals are why multi-stage rockets and orbital refueling exist as core strategies rather than niceties — no single stage needs to carry the entire mission total at once. A Duna round trip, for example, naturally splits into a Kerbin ascent stage (discarded at LKO), a transfer stage (discarded after Duna capture), and a lander that does its own landing-and-ascent leg independently.

Margin guidance

Always add margin
  • Add 10-20% extra Δv on top of the raw map numbers for correction burns, docking approaches, and landing/ascent piloting error.
  • Budget separately for "there and back" missions vs. one-way probes — a round-trip crewed lander needs the outbound legs, the return legs, and margin on both, not a single combined margin at the end.
  • Add extra margin on any leg you're new to (first Duna landing, first Jool aerobrake) — the numbers assume you execute the maneuver efficiently, and a learning curve costs real fuel.

How much margin you actually need scales with how forgiving the mission is if you come up short. A few rules of thumb:

Mission typeSuggested marginWhy
Unmanned probe, one-way~10%Worst case you fall short of a perfect orbit; no crew at risk
Crewed landing, round trip15-20%Landing and ascent errors are expensive and a shortfall strands a Kerbal
Docking-heavy station logistics+50-100 m/s flat, on top of transfer marginRCS-heavy final approach costs are hard to predict precisely in advance
First attempt at a new destination20%+You don't yet know your own execution error at this specific maneuver

Margin isn't just extra fuel tankage — it's also TWR. A stage with the right total Δv but a too-low thrust-to-weight ratio can still fail a landing by not being able to slow down fast enough before touchdown, even though the fuel gauge says there's plenty left.

Caveats and technique

These numbers assume reasonably efficient technique: good transfer window timing, aerobraking where the destination has an atmosphere, and clean burns without excessive steering losses. Your actual numbers will vary with pilot skill, rocket design, and how precisely you time your windows. A few specific sources of "invisible" Δv loss worth knowing about:

  • Gravity losses — time spent burning while gravity is also pulling you down (most relevant during a Kerbin or Eve ascent); a lower-TWR early ascent generally loses more to gravity than a snappier one, up to a point.
  • Steering losses — burning while not pointed exactly prograde/retrograde wastes part of every burn; long burns split into shorter ones bracketing the node waste less.
  • Atmospheric drag — climbing too fast, too low, in a thick atmosphere (Eve especially) burns Δv fighting air resistance rather than gaining altitude or speed.
  • Imprecise transfer windows — a "close enough" window found by eye can cost hundreds of extra m/s compared to a well-tuned one; the difference compounds on longer transfers.

For the techniques that get your real-world numbers close to these reference figures, see Interplanetary Travel for the fundamentals and Advanced Maneuvers for tightening up execution.
