Game modes: which one to pick

Before you touch a single part, KSP asks you to pick a game mode. This choice changes how much content you have access to and how much bookkeeping you have to do, but not the underlying physics — a rocket flies the same way in every mode.

ModePartsFundsContractsBest for
SandboxAll unlockedUnlimitedNonePlayers who already know the basics and just want to build
ScienceUnlocked via science treeNot trackedNoneLearning — new players, first campaign
CareerUnlocked via science treeTracked, spent on parts/buildingsYes, plus reputationA full "space agency management" layer once basics feel natural
Recommendation: start in Science mode

Science mode unlocks parts gradually as you collect science data, which naturally teaches you what each part is for instead of dumping the entire parts list on you at once. You never have to worry about running out of funds while you're still learning not to explode on the launchpad. Move to Career mode later, once staging and orbits feel routine — see Career Mode for what changes.

Touring the Space Center

The Space Center is the hub screen with clickable buildings. Each one matters at a different stage of your career — as an absolute beginner you'll mostly live in three of them, but it's worth knowing what the rest do so nothing on screen is a mystery.

  • Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB) — builds rockets for vertical launch. This is where you'll spend the vast majority of your early hours.
  • Runway / Spaceplane Hangar (SPH) — builds horizontal-takeoff spaceplanes. Ignore this until you're comfortable with basic rockets; see Aerodynamics & Flight when you're ready.
  • Tracking Station — lists every active vessel, satellite, and piece of debris you have, lets you switch control between them, and is where you plan maneuver nodes for uncrewed craft without loading into the flight scene.
  • Astronaut Complex — hire and manage Kerbals, view their experience level and traits, and (in Career) recover lost Kerbals for a fee.
  • R&D (Research & Development) — spend accumulated science points to unlock new parts on the tech tree. Only relevant in Science and Career modes.
  • Mission Control — accept and manage contracts, and unlock "strategy" style program-wide bonuses. Career mode only.
  • Administration Building — trade reputation and funds for long-term strategic bonuses (e.g. more science per experiment). Career mode only, and mostly a late-game tool.
You don't need all seven buildings on day one

If you're in Science or Sandbox mode, Mission Control, Administration, and most of the Astronaut Complex's options simply won't matter yet. Focus on VAB, Tracking Station, and R&D (if in Science mode) until you've flown a few missions.

Inside the Vehicle Assembly Building

The VAB is a 3D editor for stacking parts into a craft. A few concepts here will save you a lot of early confusion:

  • Parts sidebar — parts are organized into categories (Pods, Fuel Tanks, Engines, Control, Aerodynamics, Utility, etc.) down the left or right side. Search by name if you know what you're after.
  • Attachment nodes — parts snap together at glowing attachment points when you drag one near another. Stack attachment (top-to-bottom, on the vessel's centerline) and radial attachment (stuck to the side of another part) behave differently — full depth on this is in VAB Advanced Techniques.
  • Root part — the very first part you place becomes the root, and every other part attaches relative to it in the vessel's internal tree. For a simple rocket, start with the command pod as your root. You can reassign the root later with Alt+Left Click on another part if needed.
  • Staging list — the column of icons down the right edge of the screen. It controls the order things happen in-flight when you press Space — engine ignition, decouplers separating, parachutes deploying. Covered in full depth in Building Rockets.
  • Stats readout — click the small arrow/tab near the staging list (or the "Delta-V" tab if using a version with it built in) to expand a panel showing total mass, part count, and — critically — Δv (delta-v) and TWR (thrust-to-weight ratio) broken down per stage. Get in the habit of checking this before every launch.
What is delta-v, in one paragraph?

Delta-v (Δv) is a budget for "how much your velocity can change" using the propellant you're carrying, measured in meters per second (m/s). Every maneuver — climbing to orbit, transferring to the Mun, landing, coming home — costs a specific, fairly predictable amount of Δv. Instead of thinking "how much fuel do I need," KSP players think "how much Δv does this trip cost, and does my rocket have that much." This becomes the central planning tool starting in Orbital Mechanics and is covered from the ground up in Building Rockets.

Your first flight: a suborbital hopper

Don't try to reach orbit yet. Your first goal should be a controlled up-and-down "hop" that proves you understand staging, throttle, and landing. Here's a simple craft and flight plan:

  1. Build a tiny hopper in the VAB.

    Place a command pod (root part), a parachute on top of it, a small liquid fuel tank below the pod, and a basic engine (an LV-T30 "Reliant" is perfect — no gimbal needed for a straight-up hop) below the tank. Four parts, one stack.

  2. Check the staging list.

    You should see the engine igniting in the bottom stage and the parachute in a stage above it. If the game auto-staged something oddly, drag icons in the staging list to reorder them.

  3. Launch, then immediately press T to enable SAS.

    This keeps the rocket pointed the way it's already facing instead of tipping over from small aerodynamic wobbles.

  4. Hold Shift for full throttle and press Space to launch.

    Watch your altitude and speed on the navball readouts. Just go straight up — don't try to turn.

  5. Cut the throttle (X) once you're out of fuel or high enough for a good fall.

    The engine will flame out on its own when the tank runs dry; cutting throttle just stops the "phantom" full-throttle sound and control input.

  6. Deploy the parachute during the fall.

    Press Space again to advance to the parachute stage. Do this with plenty of altitude to spare on your first try — a few kilometers up is safer than waiting until the last second.

  7. Touch down and confirm your Kerbal survived.

    If the capsule lands or splashes down intact with the chute open, that's a complete, successful mission. Recover the vessel from the flight results screen (or right-click it in the Tracking Station) to bank any science and get your Kerbal home safely.

Kerbals, EVA, and staying alive

A Kerbal riding inside a pod is safe as long as the pod isn't destroyed and doesn't hit the ground (or the atmosphere, on reentry) too hard or too fast. A few essentials for your first missions:

  • Press 0 or use the on-screen EVA button to send a Kerbal on EVA (outside the pod) once you're safely in space or standing on a surface.
  • A Kerbal on EVA has a small monopropellant-fueled jetpack for maneuvering in vacuum — use it in short bursts, since it carries a limited supply.
  • Kerbals cannot survive unprotected reentry heat or hard surface impacts. Keep them sealed in a pod during any risky moment, and don't EVA in the middle of an atmosphere at high speed.
  • Every Kerbal has three traits: Courage and Stupidity (fixed, flavor stats that affect small things like panic behavior) and Experience, which rises as they fly missions and unlocks perks for their class (Pilots grant SAS bonuses, Engineers repair parts and boost ISRU, Scientists reset experiments and boost science return).
  • Stock KSP has no life support system — no food, water, or oxygen to manage. Kerbals can stay in space indefinitely. Life support only becomes a concern if you install a mod for it; see Tips & Mods.

Save early, save often

Don't worry about failing

Explosions are the normal way you learn this game. Every experienced player has a graveyard of Kerbals lost to bad staging, missing parachutes, or a forgotten heat shield. Get in the habit of quicksaving constantly — F5 to save, F9 to load the most recent quicksave — so that a mistake costs you thirty seconds, not an entire hour of progress. Save right before every risky moment: staging events, reentry, landing, and docking attempts.
