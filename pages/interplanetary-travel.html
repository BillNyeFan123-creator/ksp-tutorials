Interplanetary Travel

Transfer windows, ejection angles, and how to get from Kerbin to the Mun, Duna, Jool, and beyond.

Start with the Mun and Minmus

Before attempting another planet, get comfortable running a full round trip to Kerbin's own moons. Every skill an interplanetary mission needs — plotting a transfer, capturing, landing, and coming home — is exactly the same skill at a much smaller time and fuel cost, and a mistake here is far cheaper to recover from than one four months into an interplanetary coast.

  1. Get into a stable low Kerbin orbit.
  2. Set the Mun (or Minmus) as your target and place a maneuver node on your orbit.
  3. Pull the node's prograde handle until the predicted path shows an encounter with the target — a small closest-approach marker appears, or the trajectory visibly bends into the target's sphere of influence.
  4. Execute the burn at the node, then coast (time warp is fine) until you approach the target's sphere of influence.
  5. Burn retrograde near periapsis once inside the target's sphere of influence to capture into orbit, then proceed to landing as covered in Landing & Return.
Minmus is cheaper than it looks

Minmus needs a plane change most players skip on their first Mun trip (it orbits slightly inclined relative to Kerbin's equator), but its very low gravity makes landing and re-launching dramatically cheaper in Δv once you're there — a good second stop after the Mun.

What is a transfer window?

Every planet and moon is constantly moving around whatever it orbits, and all at different speeds. A transfer window is the specific stretch of time where the relative positions of your starting body and your destination line up so that a single, reasonably efficient burn now will put you on a path that meets the destination where it will be when you actually arrive — not where it is today.

Launching outside the window is expensive, not impossible

You can technically burn toward a planet at any time, but missing the window means your transfer orbit crosses the destination's orbit when the destination itself is nowhere near that point. Closing that gap costs dramatically more Δv — often far more than a normal rocket carries — so window timing isn't a nice-to-have, it's what makes the trip affordable at all.

Finding a window in-game

KSP doesn't show you a calendar of upcoming windows by default — you find one directly with the maneuver node tool, zoomed out to solar-system scale.

  1. Get into a stable low Kerbin orbit first — interplanetary transfers are planned from orbit, not from the launchpad.
  2. Set your destination planet as target in map view.
  3. Place a maneuver node and pull the prograde handle out significantly. Zoom the map view all the way out to solar-system scale so you can see your resulting solar orbit stretch toward the target's orbit.
  4. Drag the node itself forward or backward in time along your current orbit while re-adjusting the burn size, searching for the moment where your stretched trajectory's far end lines up with where the target planet will actually be — shown by an encounter marker appearing on the target's orbit line.
  5. Fine-tune the node once you have a rough encounter, nudging the prograde and any small normal component until the closest-approach distance drops inside the target's sphere of influence.
This is fiddly by hand — that's normal

Finding a clean interplanetary encounter manually takes real practice, and even experienced players spend a few minutes nudging a node. Many players also use an external transfer window planner tool to get a rough launch date before ever opening the maneuver node, then fine-tune in-game from there.

Executing the ejection burn

The burn that sends you out of Kerbin's sphere of influence and onto an interplanetary trajectory is called the ejection burn. It's most Δv-efficient when performed from a low parking orbit, burning prograde at the specific point in your orbit that aims the resulting escape trajectory correctly — that direction is the "ejection angle."

You don't need to calculate the ejection angle by hand. The maneuver node tool handles it automatically: once you've dragged the node to a point in your orbit and shaped it into a good encounter with the target planet, the game has effectively already found the correct ejection angle for you — just execute the burn the node describes.

Kerbin's own escape Δv from a typical low parking orbit is roughly 950 m/s on top of what you already spent reaching that orbit; the additional Δv needed to actually reach a given planet on top of that escape burn varies a lot by destination and is covered in detail on the Delta-v Map page.

Mid-course correction burns

Interplanetary coasts can take many months of game time — use high time warp once you're safely on your way. Partway through the trip, re-check your projected encounter in map view; it typically drifts slightly from where it was right after the ejection burn, since the maneuver node math is only an approximation at the moment you set it.

  1. Wait until you're a good fraction of the way through the coast — corrections made early are cheap but imprecise; too late and you may not have enough remaining time to react.
  2. Place a small new maneuver node partway along your current trajectory.
  3. Nudge it slightly to tighten your periapsis at the target down to exactly where you want it — low enough for capture, or aimed at a specific atmospheric entry angle if you're planning to aerobrake.
  4. Expect this to cost very little Δv compared to the ejection burn — that's the whole point of catching drift early rather than trying to fix a bad approach at the last minute.

Arrival: capture or aerobrake

  • Propulsive capture — burn retrograde near periapsis at the target to drop into a stable orbit, exactly the same technique as circularizing after launch from Kerbin, just scaled to the target body's own gravity.
  • Aerobraking — for planets and moons with an atmosphere (Duna, Eve, Jool's moon Laythe, and Kerbin itself for a return trip), you can fly your periapsis through the upper atmosphere to bleed off velocity via drag over one or more passes, instead of burning fuel to capture. This saves significant Δv but demands a heat-tolerant craft and a carefully chosen periapsis.
Aerobraking caution

Always check your craft's estimated survivable periapsis before committing to an aerobrake pass — too low and you risk overheating or structural failure; too high and the pass does essentially nothing. When in doubt, fly a shallow first pass to measure how much velocity you actually shed, then adjust deeper on a second pass rather than diving straight in blind.

Planets and moons at a glance

This is a quick-reference summary only — deeper per-body tactics (best landing sites, ascent/descent notes, common mistakes) live on the Planet-by-Planet Guide, and the full transfer Δv numbers for every destination live on the Delta-v Map.

BodyPersonality / difficulty
MohoClosest to the Sun; small and airless, but a steep orbital inclination and eccentricity make an efficient transfer genuinely tricky — a deceptively hard "early" destination.
Eve & GillyThick atmosphere and high surface gravity make Eve infamous for being far easier to land on than to leave; tiny moon Gilly is one of the cheapest landings in the game.
Duna & IkeThin atmosphere, Mars-like — the standard first interplanetary target. Supports aerobraking and parachutes; airless neighbor Ike is a cheap bonus landing from the same trip.
DresSmall, airless, and often skipped — sits in an isolated, moderately inclined orbit that makes it a good "second planet" once you're comfortable with basic transfers.
Jool & moonsA gas giant you can't land on, but its five moons (Laythe, Vall, Tylo, Bop, Pol) form an entire sub-system worth of destinations — Laythe in particular has an ocean and a landable atmosphere. A long, expensive, and rewarding trip.
EelooA distant icy dwarf planet with a long transfer time and real fuel demands — a strong "final milestone" destination once earlier planets feel routine.
