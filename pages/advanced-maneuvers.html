Gravity assists (slingshots)

A gravity assist uses a close flyby of a moving body to change your heliocentric (Sun-relative) velocity for free, without burning any fuel. It works because your speed relative to the body you fly past is unchanged by the encounter, but the body itself is moving relative to the Sun — so the same flyby that looks like a simple deflection in the body's own frame can add or subtract a large chunk of velocity in the Sun's frame.

  • Trailing-side flyby — pass behind the body relative to its direction of travel, and you pick up energy, speeding up relative to the Sun. Useful for reaching the outer system more cheaply.
  • Leading-side flyby — pass in front of the body's direction of travel, and you lose energy, slowing down relative to the Sun. Useful for dropping into the inner system, since slowing down relative to the Sun lets you fall closer in without a large retrograde burn.
Worked example: using Eve to reach Moho

Moho's low orbital radius makes it fast-moving and awkward to match orbits with directly — meeting it from a Kerbin-only transfer usually costs a heavy combined plane-change-and-capture burn. Flying an Eve leading-side flyby on the way in bleeds off heliocentric velocity for free, dropping your effective periapsis and inclination relative to Moho's orbit before you ever burn again — turning an expensive direct transfer into a cheaper multi-leg trip, at the cost of a longer flight time and a much fussier trajectory to plan.

Worked example: Jool assists for the outer system

Jool's huge mass makes it an efficient assist body for adjusting inclination or picking up a large speed change cheaply, which is why many missions headed to Eeloo — or back from Jool's own moon system — route a flyby of Jool itself into the plan rather than paying for the full plane change and speed change directly.

You'll need a fairly precise encounter

Gravity assists are sensitive to the exact flyby altitude and angle — small changes in your approach can swing the resulting trajectory a lot. Expect to iterate on the incoming encounter with a maneuver node well before the flyby, not just wing it on arrival.

Combining a plane change with another burn

A plane change (fixing orbital inclination) is one of the most expensive maneuvers in the game if done in isolation, because it means changing your velocity vector's direction rather than just its magnitude. The standard trick to reduce its cost is to combine it with a burn you were already going to make.

  • Fold it into your ejection burn. If you need both to leave a parking orbit and fix an inclination mismatch with your destination, do both at once at the ejection node — the combined vector sum is cheaper than doing a pure ejection burn and a separate pure plane-change burn back to back.
  • Do it at a node crossing. Any plane change is cheapest exactly where your current orbit crosses the target plane (the ascending or descending node) — combine the plane-change component with whatever prograde/retrograde adjustment you needed at that same point rather than waiting for a separate, dedicated pass.
The maneuver node tool does the vector math for you

You don't need trigonometry to combine burns — drag both the prograde and normal/anti-normal handles on the same node until the resulting trajectory satisfies both goals (correct plane and correct encounter) simultaneously; the total Δv shown is usually noticeably less than the sum of doing each separately.

Bi-elliptic transfers

A standard Hohmann transfer (one burn to raise/lower to the new orbit, one burn to circularize) is the most efficient way to change orbits for most ratio changes. But for a very large change in orbital size, a bi-elliptic transfer can beat it: first burn out to a deliberately overshot, very high intermediate apoapsis, adjust your orbit's periapsis (or plane) cheaply out there, then fall back and finish shaping the orbit near the original body.

The reason this can win despite the seemingly wasteful detour is that burns which change an orbit's shape or plane are cheaper the slower you're moving — and you move slowest at the farthest point from the primary body. Overshooting far out, doing the expensive-feeling shape/plane adjustment there at low speed, then returning, can cost less total Δv than trying to do the same adjustment close in at high speed, provided the ratio between the starting and final orbit is large enough.

Only worth it for large ratio changes

For small or moderate orbit changes, the detour itself costs more than it saves and a plain Hohmann transfer wins outright. Bi-elliptic transfers only pay off when the ratio between your starting and target orbit is large — think "low orbit to a very distant high orbit around the same body," not "raise apoapsis by 50 km."

Staging for long missions: motherships and cruise stages

For ambitious multi-body missions, it's often more efficient to build a reusable "mothership" — a long-endurance cruise stage that carries a separate, disposable lander — rather than a single all-in-one craft.

  • LV-N "Nerv" nuclear engine — roughly 800s of vacuum Isp at only 60 kN of thrust. Excellent for a long interplanetary cruise stage where burn time doesn't matter much, but its poor atmospheric Isp and low thrust make it a bad choice for launching off a planet or landing on one.
  • IX-6315 "Dawn" ion engine — roughly 4200s of vacuum Isp at only about 2 kN of thrust. Extremely efficient per unit of Xenon, but needs a large electric charge supply (big solar arrays or RTGs) and is only practical for very light, uncrewed, long-duration probes — not for crewed motherships or anything that needs to maneuver quickly.
  • Keep the lander separate and chemical-fueled. A conventional high-thrust engine on the lander handles the actual descent and ascent, where you need responsive thrust, while the low-thrust, high-Isp cruise stage handles the long, patient burns between bodies.
Low thrust means long burns — plan around it

A Nerv or ion cruise stage can take minutes rather than seconds to complete a single maneuver node burn. Split long burns so you start early enough that the node's midpoint lines up with the intended burn time, rather than starting exactly at the node and overshooting.

Low periapsis burns and the Oberth effect

The Oberth effect is the reason burning prograde deep in a gravity well is more effective than burning the same amount of Δv far away from a body: a rocket engine does more useful work on your orbital energy when it's already moving fast, and you move fastest at your lowest periapsis. Practically, this means:

  • Ejection burns are cheapest from a low, fast orbit rather than a high, slow one — which is part of why the standard advice is always to burn for another planet from as low a Kerbin parking orbit as your craft can tolerate.
  • Capturing at a destination benefits the same way — burning retrograde right at a low periapsis pass captures more efficiently than trying to slow down further out.
  • This shifts "optimal" burn timing and altitude toward the deepest, fastest point of whatever orbit you're in, rather than the most visually convenient one.

Powered slingshots

A powered slingshot combines a gravity assist with a burn made right at the flyby's periapsis, stacking the free velocity change from the assist with an Oberth-boosted burn at the moment you're moving fastest relative to the flyby body. This is an advanced, situational technique — mainly useful when a mission needs more of a speed change than either the assist or the burn could deliver alone, and it requires nailing the flyby geometry precisely enough that adding an engine burn during the pass doesn't ruin the encounter you already worked to set up.

When you actually need any of this

You don't need any of this to reach other planets

Every technique on this page is optional. A plain Hohmann-style transfer, the kind covered on the Interplanetary Travel page, is entirely sufficient to reach Duna, Eve, Jool, or anywhere else in the stock system with a reasonably sized rocket.

This is how experienced players go further on less fuel

Gravity assists, combined burns, bi-elliptic transfers, and Oberth-aware burn timing are how players pursuing ambitious multi-body missions — grand tours of the Jool system, awkward-orbit targets like Moho, or fully reusable interplanetary motherships — save massive amounts of Δv that a straightforward mission plan would otherwise spend. Treat them as tools to reach for once basic transfers feel routine, not as a prerequisite for leaving Kerbin's system at all.
