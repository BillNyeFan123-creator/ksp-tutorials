What ISRU actually solves

In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) means manufacturing propellant on the spot from local resources, rather than launching every drop of fuel you'll ever need from Kerbin. The problem it solves is purely one of Δv economics: fuel you need for a return trip has to be carried all the way there, which means carrying the fuel to carry that fuel, and so on — the classic rocket equation penalty. A lander that needs to both land and later launch back to orbit, then transfer home, is hauling three separate fuel budgets from the moment it leaves the pad.

ISRU breaks that chain. If a lander can produce its own ascent and return fuel from ore mined at the landing site, it only needs enough fuel to get there and land — everything needed to get home can be manufactured after arrival. This is especially valuable for:

  • Reusable landers that make repeated trips between orbit and the surface of the same body, refueling locally each time instead of being resupplied from Kerbin.
  • Long-distance return trips (Duna, Ike, and beyond) where the Δv cost of hauling return fuel the entire way out and back is enormous compared to producing it locally.
  • Refueling waypoints — a depot at the Mun or Minmus that lets outbound interplanetary craft top off cheaply before committing to the expensive part of their journey.
ISRU doesn't need an atmosphere

Ore can be mined from airless bodies just as well as atmospheric ones — the Mun, Minmus, and Ike are all common, low-gravity ISRU targets specifically because they're cheap to land on and launch from, on top of being convenient waypoints.

Scanning for ore before you land

Ore isn't distributed evenly across a planet's surface, so committing a heavy mining lander to a random landing site is a gamble. Scan first, then land.

  1. Orbital survey first. Put a probe or craft carrying the M700 Survey Scanner (or a narrow-band scanner) into a polar orbit around your target body. Running the scanner over time builds up a resource abundance map of the surface, showing where ore concentration is highest.
  2. Read the resource overlay. Once scanned, the map view can display an ore abundance overlay — look for the brightest/highest-percentage regions rather than settling for the first patch of ground you see.
  3. Confirm on the surface with a prospecting drill. Orbital scans give a low-resolution estimate. After landing (or as a small scout probe sent ahead of your main mining lander), use a PresQ Core Drill or the surface-mounted prospecting instrument to get an exact, local ore concentration reading before committing your main rig to that spot.
  4. Pick your final site based on the confirmed reading. Ore concentration directly affects how fast your drill produces ore, so a site confirmed at high concentration is worth walking or hopping a rover a short distance to reach, rather than settling for a mediocre spot.
Scout before you commit the whole rig

If you're not sure a body's ore deposits are worth it, send a lightweight scout probe with just a scanner and a prospecting drill first. It's much cheaper to lose or reposition a small scout than to discover your fully-loaded mining lander is sitting on a low-yield patch of ground.

The mining rig equipment list

A functioning ISRU rig needs four categories of part working together — miss one and the chain stalls.

StorageOre tanks
ConverterConvert-O-Tron 250
CoolingRadiators
  • Drill-O-Matic (or the smaller Junior version): the mining part itself. It must be deployed/"anchored" to the ground before it will operate — right-click and extend it, the same way you'd extend landing legs, and it needs solid contact with terrain rather than hovering. It produces ore faster over higher-concentration deposits and, to a lesser extent, when the surrounding surface is more solid.
  • Ore storage tanks: hold the mined ore until the converter processes it. Ore is fairly dense, so tanks fill up faster than you'd expect relative to their size — plan for enough buffer capacity that a brief converter shutdown (e.g. running out of power) doesn't immediately overflow the drill's output.
  • Convert-O-Tron 250: the ISRU conversion unit. It consumes ore and electric charge to produce Liquid Fuel, Oxidizer, or Monopropellant — you choose which output(s) to run in its right-click menu. Conversion isn't perfectly efficient: some of the ore's mass is lost as waste heat and inefficiency rather than converting 1:1 into propellant mass.
  • Radiators: the converter generates a large amount of waste heat, and it throttles itself down automatically if it can't shed that heat fast enough. Deployable radiators (or fixed radiator panels for smaller rigs) are effectively mandatory if you want the converter running at full rate rather than a fraction of its rated output.
Don't forget the electric charge cost

The Convert-O-Tron 250 draws a substantial amount of electric charge continuously while running. A rig with plenty of ore and a fully deployed drill will still sit idle — or run at a fraction of capacity — if it can't cover the converter's power draw. Size your power system for the converter, not just the drill.

Power requirements

ISRU converters are power-hungry by design, which makes power generation the real bottleneck on most mining rigs — more often than ore concentration or drill count.

  • Solar arrays are the cheapest option in terms of launched mass, but they only produce power in daylight, and output drops sharply with distance from the Sun (a panel that's plenty for a Mun rig is nearly useless at Jool or Eeloo without oversizing dramatically).
  • RTGs (Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators) produce a small but constant trickle of power regardless of sunlight, distance from the Sun, or time of day — ideal for high-latitude sites, bodies with long nights, or deep-space locations where solar is impractical. They're heavier and more expensive per unit of power than solar, so they're usually used to supplement solar rather than replace it entirely, except on rigs that must run through extended darkness.
  • Batteries buffer power through eclipses or local night, letting a mostly-solar rig keep the converter running (or at least not fully stall) during the dark half of an orbit or a planet's night cycle.

A rule of thumb for a fixed surface rig: size solar for comfortably more than the converter's peak draw during daylight, add enough battery capacity to ride out the local night without the converter fully shutting down, and add RTGs if the site's latitude or the body's distance from the Sun makes solar unreliable.

Building a fuel depot lander, step by step

  1. Confirm your landing site. Use the orbital scan plus surface prospecting approach above to pick a high-concentration ore site before you commit the lander's descent burn.
  2. Land near — not necessarily on — the confirmed spot. A precise landing helps, but a rover or short hop can close the last stretch if you land nearby instead of exactly on the reading.
  3. Deploy the drill and radiators. Right-click both and extend/deploy them. The drill needs solid ground contact; radiators need to be angled or positioned so they aren't blocked by other parts of the craft.
  4. Confirm power is positive. Check your electric charge trend once the drill and converter are both running — if charge is draining, throttle back the converter's output or wait for more daylight before running it at full rate.
  5. Run the converter and let ore tanks fill and drain. Set the Convert-O-Tron to produce the propellant type(s) you need (typically Liquid Fuel + Oxidizer for a chemical engine return stage). This step just takes real time — leave the rig running and time-warp forward.
  6. Transfer produced fuel into the lander's main tanks. If the ISRU equipment is mounted on a separate tank stack from your ascent stage, use fuel transfer (right-click tanks, or the resource transfer UI) to move Liquid Fuel/Oxidizer into the tanks that actually feed your ascent engine.
  7. Either launch on the now-refueled lander, or wait for a visiting craft. A self-contained depot lander can top off its own tanks and fly itself home; a dedicated depot (not meant to fly again) instead waits for other craft to rendezvous, dock, and pull fuel via a fuel duct or docking port resource transfer.
Time-warp is your friend here

ISRU production rates are usually slow relative to a single orbit or day — don't sit and watch it happen in real time. Once the rig is confirmed stable (positive power, drill anchored, converter running), switch to another vessel or warp forward and check back periodically.

Practical use cases

Mun or Minmus depot as a Kerbin-system waypoint

A permanent ISRU rig at the Mun or Minmus (Minmus's low gravity makes it especially cheap to land on and launch from) lets outbound craft top off their tanks after leaving Kerbin but before committing to the expensive part of an interplanetary transfer. This effectively lets you launch heavier or less-than-full interplanetary craft from Kerbin, saving on launch costs, then finish fueling in a much cheaper gravity well.

Duna or Ike depot for a self-sufficient return trip

Carrying enough fuel from Kerbin for a full round trip to Duna is expensive. Landing an ISRU rig (often more practical on Ike, thanks to its much lower gravity and lack of atmosphere) lets a mission produce its own return fuel on-site instead, turning what would be a very heavy one-shot mission profile into a lighter outbound trip plus a local refueling stop before heading home.

When ISRU is (and isn't) worth it

Mining rigs are heavy and slow to pay off

A full ISRU setup — drill, ore tanks, converter, radiators, and enough power generation to run it all — adds significant mass and part count to a mission, and meaningful fuel production takes real game time, not a couple of minutes. For a one-off short hop (a quick Mun landing and return, for example) it's almost always cheaper in total mass and complexity to just bring enough fuel from Kerbin than to bring a mining rig you'll only use once.

ISRU pays off best when the investment gets reused: a reusable lander making repeated trips, a long-term base, or a return trip so Δv-expensive that hauling all the fuel from Kerbin isn't realistic in the first place. Treat "will I use this rig more than once, or is this trip's return cost otherwise brutal?" as the deciding question before adding ISRU equipment to a craft.
