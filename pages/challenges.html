Jool-5

Difficulty: Extreme. Land on and return from all five moons of Jool — Laythe, Vall, Tylo, Bop, and Pol — in a single mission.

What makes it hard isn't any one landing, it's the combination: Tylo alone demands a Kerbin-launch- grade Δv budget for landing and ascent because it's airless with Kerbin-level gravity, Laythe needs aero-capable gear (wings, chutes, or both) that the other four moons don't, and you're managing one mothership plus potentially several single-use or reusable landers across a mission that can run into the hundreds of in-game days.

Toughest legTylo (land + ascend)
Easiest legsPol, Bop
Special gear neededLaythe aero kit

Sequencing matters as much as hardware. Many attempts land on Pol and Bop first, since both are cheap and low-risk, banking easy wins and confirming the mothership's systems all work before committing to the two expensive, high-stakes legs at Tylo and Laythe. Tylo is usually saved for when the mothership still has plenty of contingency fuel left, since it has the least margin for error of any leg in the mission.

A workable approach

Most successful Jool-5 runs use a large mothership carrying one Tylo-capable lander (built to a near-Kerbin-launch spec) and a separate, lighter reusable lander for the other four moons, refueled between hops if you're bringing ISRU equipment. See Advanced Maneuvers for efficient multi-moon rendezvous planning and ISRU & Mining if you want to refuel in-system instead of carrying everything from Kerbin.

Don't underestimate the mission clock

Between transfer windows, orbital adjustments inside the Jool system, and five separate landings, a Jool-5 attempt commonly runs 300+ in-game days from launch to splashdown back at Kerbin. Bring enough electric charge generation and life support margin (if you're running a life-support mod) for a mission that long, not just enough Δv.

Grand Tour

Difficulty: High (orbit-only) to Extreme (full landings). Visit every body in the Kerbol system in one continuous mission. "Visit" is commonly interpreted as achieving orbit at every body as the minimum bar; landing on every landable body is the recognized "hard mode" version of the same challenge.

The main difficulty is mission length and Δv stacking — you're planning a route through bodies with wildly different orbital geometry (Moho and Eeloo especially) in a single craft or mothership that has to carry enough margin for the whole trip. See the Planet-by-Planet Guide for a suggested visiting order that tackles the easy bodies first and saves Moho and Eeloo for last.

Most Grand Tour attempts are built around a single large mothership with a nuclear-engine transfer stage, carrying one or more general-purpose landers that get refueled or swapped out as the mission goes on. Because the mission spans essentially the entire game's content, it's common to treat a Grand Tour as a capstone project attempted only after you've already done Jool-5 and an Eve return individually — trying all of it for the first time in one uninterrupted mission is a lot to debug at once.

Orbit-only counts as a legitimate first attempt

If landing on all fifteen non-Kerbin bodies feels overwhelming, an orbit-only Grand Tour is still a serious, respected achievement on its own, and a natural stepping stone toward the full landing version later.

Eve Return

Difficulty: Extreme — widely considered the single hardest stock challenge. Land on Eve's surface, then launch back off to orbit and return the craft (ideally with its Kerbal alive) to Kerbin.

The difficulty is almost entirely in the ascent: Eve's thick atmosphere plus high gravity means the rocket that already had to land gently now needs a Δv budget that can exceed a full Kerbin launch, and it has to carry that entire ascent stage down to the surface first without it being destroyed on landing.

Two common design approaches
  • Very high part-count asparagus staging — a tall, heavily-staged ascent vehicle that drops empty tanks continuously on the way up, maximizing effective ISP throughout the climb.
  • A low, wide lander that carries its own compact ascent rocket — prioritizes landing stability and a shorter, more survivable touchdown, accepting a slightly less optimal ascent shape in exchange for not tipping over on arrival.

See the Eve entry in the Planet-by-Planet Guide and the Delta-v Map for the specific numbers you're budgeting against.

SSTO spaceplane challenges

Difficulty: Moderate to High. Build a single-stage-to-orbit spaceplane — reusable, no dropped stages — that takes off from a runway, reaches a stable orbit, and returns to land in one piece. Variants include payload-fraction competitions (how much cargo can you deliver to orbit and back) and round-trip challenges to Laythe, the only other stock body suited to the same flight profile.

Getting the intake-to-engine ratio and ascent profile right for hybrid air-breathing/rocket propulsion is the core skill here. See Aerodynamics & Flight for the fundamentals of building a craft that flies efficiently both in atmosphere and out of it.

Common variantMax payload fraction
Harder variantRound trip to Laythe
Key skillIntake/engine tuning

A common failure mode is an SSTO that flies beautifully in atmosphere but can't finish circularizing once air-breathing intakes stop producing thrust near the edge of the atmosphere — make sure your closed-cycle rocket mode alone has enough Δv to complete the orbit, not just get you up to the edge of space. The reverse failure — plenty of rocket Δv but not enough wing area or intake air to get through the thick lower atmosphere efficiently — is just as common on early attempts.

No Kerbal Left Behind

Difficulty: Self-imposed, scales with your risk tolerance. Play with Kerbal respawn turned off and treat every mission as though deaths are permanent — no reverting to launch after a bad decision costs a crew, no shrugging off a botched landing.

This isn't a single mission to complete so much as a standing ruleset for players who want higher stakes on every launch. It pairs well with any of the challenges above — attempting Jool-5 or an Eve return under a no-respawn rule raises the tension considerably, since a single mistake becomes permanent instead of just expensive.

A lighter version some players prefer: keep respawn on, but adopt a personal rule that a Kerbal death still "counts" against the mission (write it in the mission report, retire the vessel design, whatever gives it weight) without the harsher permanence of losing them from the roster forever. The goal either way is the same — making risk feel real again once quicksaving and reverts have made early failures essentially free.

A few more community classics

Beyond the headline challenges above, a few other long-running community favorites are worth knowing about once you're looking for more structured goals:

  • Kerbin flag-planting tour — plant a flag in every biome of a single body (often Kerbin itself, or the Mun) using a rover or a series of short hops, a good low-stakes way to practice precision landings without leaving the home system.
  • Payload fraction competitions — maximize the ratio of payload mass to total launch mass for a delivery to a fixed target orbit, a good complement to the SSTO challenge below if you'd rather optimize a conventional staged rocket instead.
  • Space station part-count or mass milestones — build and maintain a station past a self-set threshold (say, 100 parts, or a full crew complement of 12), which tests your docking and logistics skills as much as any single flight does.
  • Asteroid redirect and capture — track down, rendezvous with, and tow a wandering asteroid into a stable Kerbin orbit using a claw part, a puzzle in orbital mechanics and docking precision rather than raw Δv.

None of these carry quite the same reputation as Jool-5, the Grand Tour, or an Eve return, but they're excellent for keeping a save interesting between attempts at the bigger goals.

Personal milestones for newer players

Not ready for the community classics above? That's normal — they assume you've already got the fundamentals down cold. Here's a natural progression through this site's own chapters that builds toward them:

  1. First orbit. Get any craft into a stable orbit around Kerbin and back down in one piece. Covered in Getting Started and Building Rockets.
  2. First Mun landing. Land on and return from the Mun (or Minmus, which is genuinely easier — see the Planet-by-Planet Guide).
  3. First successful docking. Rendezvous and dock two craft in orbit without a collision. See Rendezvous & Docking.
  4. First working space station. Assemble a multi-module station from separate launches. See Space Stations.
  5. First interplanetary landing. Duna is the standard first target — thin atmosphere, aerobraking assist, and a moderate Δv budget. See Interplanetary Travel and Landing & Return.
Once you've cleared all five

You've got the core skill set for every challenge on this page — orbital rendezvous, multi-launch assembly, interplanetary transfers, and atmospheric landings. Jool-5, the Grand Tour, an Eve return, or your first SSTO are all reasonable next targets from here.

A few intermediate milestones worth adding to the list once the five above feel routine, as a bridge toward the community classics: a landing on Duna's moon Ike in the same mission as the main Duna landing, a successful aerobrake capture (at Duna or Jool) instead of a fully propulsive one, a crewed round trip that brings a Kerbal home alive from an interplanetary landing rather than just a probe, and a station or base that's been kept crewed and running across more than one game year. None of these need new hardware concepts beyond what the five core milestones already taught you — they're really about combining and refining skills you already have.
