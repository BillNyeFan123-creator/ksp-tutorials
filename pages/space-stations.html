Why build modular

Launching an entire station in one piece runs into a hard ceiling fast: your biggest rocket can only lift so much mass and so much part-count before it becomes unstable or unbuildable. Building modular instead — launching one self-contained piece at a time and docking it to what's already up there — lets a station grow indefinitely, spreads cost and risk across multiple smaller launches instead of one all-or-nothing one, and lets you design, test, and launch each module's specific job (habitat, lab, fuel) separately rather than compromising one giant rocket to do everything.

Smaller launches fail smaller

If a single-launch station design has a structural or staging failure on the way up, you lose the whole station. If one module in a modular plan fails, you lose that module and try again — the rest of the station, already in orbit, is unaffected.

Designing the core module

The first piece you launch becomes the anchor everything else docks to, so it needs to be self-sufficient from day one:

  • Control — a probe core (for uncrewed operation before crew arrives) or a crewed pod, ideally both; a probe core on the station means you can still control it for docking maneuvers even between crew rotations.
  • A multi-port docking hub — a part with several docking ports arranged around it (or several individual ports mounted radially) so multiple future modules can attach without fighting over the same spot.
  • Batteries and solar panels sized for the core's own systems plus headroom for whatever docks later, since new modules often draw power from the shared station grid rather than carrying enough of their own.
  • RCS and reaction wheels for fine attitude control — a large, asymmetric station is much harder to rotate than a single ship, so generous RCS thruster placement (and monopropellant supply) pays off.

Adding modules over time

  1. Design a single-purpose module — a habitat (crew capacity), a lab (science processing), a fuel depot (large tankage plus a docking port), or a power module (extra solar/RTG capacity) — each with its own docking port sized to match the core.
  2. Launch to a matching orbit — same altitude and inclination as the station, timed so the module passes near the station shortly after reaching orbit (see the launch-timing technique on the Docking & Rendezvous page).
  3. Rendezvous and dock to an open port on the hub.
  4. Repeat, planning ahead for which port each future module will use so the station doesn't end up with modules blocking each other's docking approach paths.

Station systems in depth

Power balance

Add up total power draw across every docked module (probe cores, labs, lights, transmitting antennas) against total generation capacity. The critical case is eclipse periods — time spent in the body's shadow with zero solar input — during which the station survives purely on battery charge. Size battery capacity to cover your worst-case eclipse duration at your orbital altitude, not just average power draw.

Structural rigidity

Long docked chains of modules can wobble or even snap apart under thrust or timewarp physics loading, especially with heavy, off-center modules. Keep added mass as close to the station's center as practical, avoid extremely long unsupported docking chains, and use autostrut (available on most structural and docking parts in the part right-click menu) to rigidly brace modules against the station's root part or heaviest part, cutting down on visible flex and part-breaking stress.

Fuel storage and transfer

A dedicated fuel depot module with large tanks lets visiting ships refuel without carrying their own return fuel margin the whole way up. Right-click a fuel tank on either docked craft and choose transfer resource (or use the in-game resource transfer window) to move fuel, monopropellant, or other resources between any two parts connected through a docking port.

Crew capacity and rotation

Track total crew capacity across all habitat modules versus how many Kerbals are actually stationed there. Plan a rotation schedule (bringing fresh crew up, returning tired crew down) using a reusable crew shuttle rather than a one-way capsule, so the same vehicle can serve the station indefinitely.

Practical station shapes

ShapeProsCons
Linear / stackSimple to build, easy to keep symmetric, straightforward mass distributionDocking new modules to the ends only; can get long and structurally floppy
Hub-and-spoke (multi-port hub)Many modules can dock without blocking each other; compact, rigid center of massHub part itself becomes a single point of failure; port arrangement must be planned ahead

Most real stations end up as a hybrid: a hub-and-spoke core for the first several modules, with one or two of those spokes extended into short linear stacks (for example, a fuel depot with two or three tank segments in a row) once the main hub ports are full. Whichever shape you pick, try to keep the overall mass roughly centered on the core — a station that's heavy on one side takes more RCS and reaction wheel authority to rotate cleanly, and puts more stress on the docking ports nearest the imbalance during timewarp.

Leave room to maneuver

Long spokes or modules mounted too close together can block a future ship's final docking approach. When planning hub port assignments, leave at least one clear, unobstructed approach lane, and consider reserving one hub port specifically for visiting ships rather than filling every port with a permanent module.

Crew and resupply logistics

  • Reusable crew shuttle — a small crewed craft with just enough Δv to reach the station and return to Kerbin, refueled and reflown for each rotation rather than left docked permanently and wasted.
  • EVA transfers — a Kerbal on EVA can move directly between two docked craft's hatches without a dedicated transfer vehicle, useful for quick crew swaps between adjacent modules without undocking anything.
  • Resupply ships — an uncrewed craft carrying fuel, monopropellant, or spare parts, docked to the depot module, resources transferred over, then either left as extra storage or undocked and deorbited to clear the port for the next resupply run.

Worked example: a first station

Core module

Probe core, 4-6 way docking hub, 4x deployable solar panels, 2 battery packs, RCS thruster quad, monopropellant tank.

Habitat module

2-4 person crew cabin, docking port matching the hub, small battery buffer, a handful of lights and a ladder/hatch for EVA access.

Lab module

Mobile Processing Lab part, docking port, extra solar panel pair (labs draw noticeably more power than most parts), battery buffer.

Fuel module

Large liquid fuel/oxidizer tanks, docking port, no engine needed (the depot doesn't fly anywhere), small RCS quad for its own docking approach when delivered.

Launch order: core first (it needs to survive alone), then habitat (so crew rotation can start), then lab and fuel module in either order depending on which contracts or science goals you're chasing first.

Send the core up slightly over-built

Because the core has to function alone for at least one full orbit (and often much longer, if the habitat launch slips), give it a little more battery, monopropellant, and solar capacity than the bare minimum. It's cheap insurance against having to babysit a barely-surviving station core while you wait for the next launch window.

Science labs

Labs turn stored data into bonus science over time

The Mobile Processing Lab can "process" science data stored aboard it, slowly converting it into extra science points over real time (accelerated by warp) as long as it's crewed by a scientist and has power. Bring collected science data to the lab via crew EVA transfer or by docking the craft that collected it, then leave the lab running — checking back periodically to collect the bonus output and reset it with a fresh crew report or experiment cycle so it keeps generating value instead of sitting idle.
