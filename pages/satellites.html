Probe cores vs. crewed pods

Every controllable vessel needs a "brain" — something that lets the game's control inputs (SAS, throttle, staging, maneuver execution) actually steer the craft. For a crewed vessel that's a command pod with a Kerbal inside. For a satellite it's a probe core: an unmanned part like the Stayputnik, RC-001S Remote Guidance Unit, or OKTO2 that does the same job without a seat.

Probe cores are lighter and usually cheaper than pods, which matters a lot when every kilogram costs Δv. The trade-off is that a probe core is entirely dependent on two things a crewed pod can partially do without: electric charge, and (if CommNet is enabled, which it is by default in Career and can be toggled in Sandbox) a signal path home. A crewed pod with a pilot on board can still fly on internal SAS with no signal at all; most probe cores cannot act on new commands without a connection, though they can hold a pre-set attitude and continue an already-started burn.

Why a Kerbal still matters, even on a "probe" mission

Some science experiments — crew reports and EVA surface samples — can only be produced by a Kerbal physically present. A pure probe can carry every instrument in the game and still never generate those two categories. See Science & Research for the full breakdown.

Power budgeting: batteries, solar, and RTGs

Almost every active part on a satellite draws a small trickle of electric charge (EC) just to stay on — the probe core itself, reaction wheels, transmitting antennas (a large one-time cost while actually sending data), and any running science instruments. If EC hits zero, you lose control: no SAS, no reaction wheel authority, no engine gimbal, and transmissions stall until charge recovers. Power design is really a simple inequality you need to satisfy over an entire orbit, including the dark part.

  1. Add up your steady draw. Check each part's EC/s consumption in the VAB part tooltip — probe core, reaction wheels, and any "always on" instruments (a running gravioli detector draws continuously; most others only draw briefly when you trigger them).
  2. Estimate your eclipse time. A low circular orbit around Kerbin spends a meaningful fraction of every lap in Kerbin's shadow, where solar panels produce nothing. Higher and more inclined orbits generally spend less time eclipsed, but never assume zero.
  3. Size your battery bank to survive the eclipse alone — battery capacity should comfortably exceed (steady draw × eclipse duration), not just match it exactly.
  4. Size your solar panels to more than replace what you used in the sunlit part of the orbit, so the battery actually recovers to full before the next eclipse rather than slowly draining lap after lap.

Deployment timing kills panels

Deployable solar panels snap off if extended into an airstream or slammed by a decoupler event, stage separation debris, or a docking collision. Never deploy panels before you're clear of the atmosphere, and retract or shield them (with a fairing, or simply by not deploying yet) around any docking or staging event where something could clip them.

Dead battery, dead satellite

A satellite that runs flat in eclipse doesn't just pause — it can drift off its intended attitude, miss a scheduled transmission, or fail to hold a targeted orbit correction. Always over-provision battery capacity beyond your worst-case eclipse estimate, especially for anything going somewhere with long orbital periods or extended shadow time, like a high Kerbin orbit or an outer-system probe.

Antennas and CommNet

With CommNet active, a probe core needs an unbroken signal path back to a tracking station (or, on an upgraded Space Center, out to increasingly distant ranges) to accept new commands. Losing connection doesn't destroy the craft, but it does freeze it to automated behavior only — it'll keep executing an in-progress maneuver node burn, but you can't queue a new one, switch SAS modes, or trigger most actions until signal is restored.

  • Direct antennas (Communotron 16, Communotron 16-S, HG-5) talk straight back to Kerbin or another tracking point. They have limited range and are fine for craft that stay relatively close to Kerbin or that can wait for a relay to swing into view.
  • Relay antennas (Communotron 88-88, HG-55, RA-2, RA-15) do everything a direct antenna does and can pass other craft's signals along toward home. A network of relays is what lets a probe on the far side of the Mun, or out at Jool, stay in constant contact even when Kerbin itself isn't in view.
  • Line of sight is everything. A signal is blocked by any sufficiently large body physically between the transmitting craft and the receiver — your own Mun, for instance, blocks a lander on its far side from reaching Kerbin directly. A relay positioned to see both the lander and Kerbin (or another relay) bridges that gap.
  • Signal strength affects data rate and, at the margins, can affect the science value returned by transmission — a weak, long-range link isn't just slower, it can be flakier.
Build the relay network before you need it

It's much easier to launch 2-3 dedicated relay satellites into a stable constellation early than to retrofit coverage after your science probes are already stranded out of contact. Treat a relay network around any body you'll visit repeatedly (Mun, Minmus, Duna) as its own small project.

Choosing an orbit for the job

There's no single "correct" satellite orbit — the right choice depends entirely on what the satellite is for. Match the orbit shape to the mission before you ever hit launch.

Orbit typeShapeBest for
Low circularNear-equatorial, low altitudeCheap deployment, quick low/high-space science passes, short-range comm
Polar / sun-synchronous-styleNear-90° inclinationSurface mapping and scanning — passes over every latitude band as the body rotates underneath
High circular / geostationaryEquatorial, altitude matched to rotation periodConstant single-region coverage, long-lived relay anchor
Highly ellipticalLow periapsis, very high apoapsisCheap way to linger far out for long comm range or a Molniya-style high-latitude relay
Rule of thumb: if the mission is "cover one fixed spot forever," go geostationary. If it's "eventually see every point on the surface," go polar. If it's "just needs to exist cheaply and relay occasionally," a low or moderate circular orbit is often good enough and far cheaper in Δv.

Building a geostationary relay

A geostationary satellite sits above the same point on Kerbin's equator permanently, because its orbital period exactly matches Kerbin's rotation period (roughly 6 hours). A geosynchronous-but-inclined orbit matches the period but not the equatorial plane, tracing a north-south figure-eight over one longitude instead of holding perfectly still — still useful, just not "stationary."

  1. Launch to a low circular parking orbit first, as normal, ideally already close to equatorial to save a later plane change.
  2. Zero out inclination. Burn at the ascending or descending node to flatten your orbit to the equator (0° inclination) — do this while still low, since plane changes get more expensive the faster you're moving, and you're moving fastest close to Kerbin... except right at the node itself, where it's unavoidable. The point is to do it once, early, rather than fight it again later at a different altitude.
  3. Raise apoapsis with a prograde burn until the orbit info panel shows an orbital period matching Kerbin's rotation period (~6 hours, check the exact value shown in-game since it can vary slightly with game settings).
  4. Circularize at that apoapsis so the whole orbit — not just one point — matches the target period.
  5. Fine-tune with small prograde/retrograde nudges. Matching the period exactly is finicky; small burns at apoapsis correct small period mismatches without disturbing the orbit shape much.
Small period errors compound over time

A satellite whose period is even slightly off from Kerbin's rotation will slowly drift east or west relative to the ground, taking days or weeks of game time to become noticeable. Check back after a few in-game days and nudge again if it's crept off station.

The 3-relay, 120° constellation

Three geostationary relays spaced evenly around the equator — 120° apart from each other — give full, constant line-of-sight coverage of the entire equatorial band and most of the globe besides, since each relay can see roughly a third of the planet at once from that altitude. This is the classic first "real" network project in a CommNet-enabled career, and it permanently solves signal coverage for anything you later send to low Kerbin orbit or beyond.

Launching all three efficiently

Launch all three relays on a shared parking orbit, then use maneuver nodes to raise each one to the geostationary shell at a different point in the orbit — since they started spread out along the same low orbit, raising each one at a different location naturally spaces them out near 120° once circularized. Alternatively, launch one at a time and use the phase angle between it and the previous relay (visible in map view) to time each subsequent launch.

Satellite contracts in Career mode

Career mode frequently offers contracts asking you to place a satellite (sometimes contract-supplied, sometimes "any craft with a probe core") into a specific orbit, defined as ranges for altitude, inclination, and eccentricity rather than one exact number.

  1. Read the required ranges carefully in the contract details — they're given as minimum/maximum bands, not single values, so you have room to aim for the middle rather than an exact figure.
  2. Plan the orbit with a maneuver node the same way you would any other target orbit — set apoapsis/periapsis for the altitude band, and handle inclination with a plane-change burn at a node crossing.
  3. Watch the live contract parameter indicators in the contract app or the in-flight contract tracker — each requirement (altitude, inclination, eccentricity) usually shows a green check or red cross that updates in real time as your orbit changes, so you can nudge burns until everything goes green before finalizing.
  4. Hold the orbit briefly if required — some contracts require the orbit to be stable for a short duration before they register as complete, so don't immediately switch vessels away the instant the indicators turn green.

Science instruments: a quick preview

Beyond communication duty, probes are also the backbone of the science game — most instruments don't need a Kerbal at all. A quick preview of what you'll find in the tech tree:

  • Thermometer — reads ambient temperature, resettable, cheap and light.
  • Barometer — reads atmospheric pressure, only useful on bodies with an atmosphere.
  • Gravioli detector — measures local gravity, draws EC continuously while active.
  • Seismometer — best used landed, benefits from time spent recording after being deployed.
  • Materials bay and Mystery Goo canister — one-use-per-refresh experiments that need to be reset (by a Kerbal on EVA, or automatically with certain tech) before they can be run again in a new biome.

Every one of these interacts with the "situation × biome" system, transmission-vs-return value, and the tech tree in ways that deserve their own deep dive — head to Science & Research for the full picture on getting the most out of every instrument you fly.
