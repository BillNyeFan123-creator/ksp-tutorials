Do you need any DLC?

No. Stock KSP — no expansions at all — is a complete, fully playable game. Every launch, every orbit, every interplanetary transfer, and every landing covered elsewhere on this site (from Getting Started through Landing & Return) works with zero DLC installed. If you're on a budget or just not sure whether you'll stick with KSP, skip both expansions for now — you won't hit a wall.

What DLC changes vs. what it doesn't

Both expansions are additive. They add new parts, new tools, and new things to do — they don't change orbital mechanics, don't gate career progression behind a paywall, and don't touch the tech tree balance in ways that make stock parts obsolete. Think of them as extra toy-box contents, not a different game.

Making History in depth

Making History is built around two things: the Mission Builder, and a set of extra parts.

The Mission Builder

This is a node-based scripting tool for authoring custom missions with defined objectives, rewards, and briefings — separate from the normal Career/Science/Sandbox game modes. You place nodes for events (reach an altitude, dock two craft, recover a vessel, keep a Kerbal alive for X days, and dozens more) and wire them into success/failure conditions and funds/reputation/science rewards. Finished missions can be played in any save and shared as files with other players.

  • Good for building your own structured challenges (e.g., "launch this exact rocket and land within 500m of a marker") rather than relying on the open-ended stock contract system.
  • Good for replaying historically-styled missions — early suborbital hops, first-orbit attempts, first docking — with explicit pass/fail criteria instead of just eyeballing it yourself.
  • Entirely optional to ever touch — you can own Making History and never open the Mission Builder, and nothing about normal play changes.

Extra parts

Making History also adds a batch of parts styled after real early-spaceflight hardware (capsules and engines with a distinct historical look) plus additional stock-alike parts that slot into the existing tech tree. These are functionally comparable to stock parts of similar tier — they widen your aesthetic and part-count options more than they unlock new capability.

Who benefits most

Players who enjoy scripting scenarios, running mission packs shared by the community, or building their own "campaign" of objectives get the most value here. If you mostly play open sandbox or standard career/science, the Mission Builder will likely sit unused.

Breaking Ground in depth

Breaking Ground is the more broadly useful expansion for most players, because it adds capability that stock parts simply don't have: things that move, and ways to survey and construct in the field.

Robotic parts

Hinges, pistons, rotors, and rotation servos let you build mechanisms with actual controlled motion, driven by action groups or the part's own tweakable settings (speed, range, torque):

  • Rover suspension and steering — proper springy suspension arms instead of stock wheels' fixed geometry, useful for rough-terrain rovers on the Mun or Duna.
  • Deployable solar panels and antenna arrays — fold flat for launch, then unfold via hinge/rotor once in orbit, useful for large stations where static parts would clip.
  • Robotic arms — multi-segment hinge/piston assemblies for grabbing, positioning, or docking-assist tasks.
  • Custom-motion cargo bays and fairings — open on a hinge instead of a canned animation, so you control the exact angle and timing.

Deployable science instruments

A set of science parts (seismometer, atmospheric sensor, weather station, and others) can be planted on a surface and left there indefinitely, rather than being a one-shot experiment you run once and discard. You can revisit the same instrument, or just leave it broadcasting, and collect additional science from it over time — useful for long-term surface outposts that keep paying off after the crew that placed them has moved on.

What you can actually build with the robotic parts

HingeRotates around one axis through a set angle rangeFolding wings, opening doors/bay lids, tilting solar arrays

RotorSpins continuously, torque and speed adjustableHelicopter-style rotors, spinning habitats, drills

These combine — a robotic arm is typically several hinges and a piston chained together, and a folding solar array might use a hinge for the deployment angle and a rotation servo to keep tracking the sun afterward. All of them are controllable through the part action window, action groups, or the dedicated robotic controller part for more complex sequenced motion.

Surface features and anomalies

Breaking Ground adds a survey scanner part you can fly or drive around to detect surface features and anomalies on a body — points of interest worth a dedicated visit, similar in spirit to stock's existing hidden Easter-egg anomalies but expanded and discoverable through normal gameplay instead of by looking up coordinates online.

EVA construction mode

This is arguably the single biggest practical addition in either DLC: a Kerbal on EVA can enter a construction mode and attach, detach, or reposition most parts directly on a craft, whether it's in orbit or sitting on a surface.

Why this matters for station and ship building

Without EVA construction, on-orbit assembly means every module needs its own docking port, and ports you no longer need stay attached forever as dead weight. With EVA construction, a Kerbal can bolt a part straight onto a hull, swap a broken antenna, or rearrange a cluster of science parts without a docking port anywhere in sight — a huge simplification for modular stations built across multiple launches. See Space Stations for the base-game modular approach this technique complements.

Which one should you buy?

If you can only pick one, most players get more day-to-day mileage out of Breaking Ground. It expands what you can physically build — rovers with real suspension, deployable arrays, robotic arms, long-term surface science, and EVA construction — and every one of those tools is useful in a completely normal career or sandbox save without any extra setup. It's the bigger addition to your "sandbox toy box."

Making History is worth it specifically if you want to build and share scripted mission scenarios, run community mission packs, or enjoy designing objective-driven challenges rather than open-ended play. If that doesn't sound like you, its extra parts alone are a much smaller draw.

Both are compatible with everything else on this site

None of the other tutorials assume you own either DLC. Where a DLC tool makes a task meaningfully easier, the relevant page mentions it briefly and links back here for depth.

Using both together

The two DLCs don't overlap or conflict — Making History's Mission Builder and parts, and Breaking Ground's robotics and EVA tools, live in completely separate systems and can both be installed at once without any interaction to worry about. If budget allows and you're already sure KSP has hooked you, owning both gives you the widest possible toolkit: script a custom mission in the Mission Builder that specifically calls for deploying a Breaking Ground robotic arm or planting a long-term science station, for example. That said, neither expansion requires the other, and plenty of players happily own just one or neither.

A reasonable buying order for most people: start with no DLC, play through Getting Started, your first Mun landing, and your first docking. If by that point you find yourself wanting more physical build variety (rover suspension, deployable arrays, on-orbit repair), add Breaking Ground. If instead you find yourself wanting to design specific scripted scenarios to share with friends or a community, add Making History. There's no wrong order, and no rush — both expansions work identically whether you buy them on day one or after a hundred hours of stock play.

Common questions

QuestionAnswer
Do I need either DLC to reach any planet in the game? No. Every body in the Kerbol system, including the outer planets and moons, is reachable in pure stock. DLC parts don't unlock new destinations.
Does Breaking Ground add new celestial bodies? No — it adds surface features and anomalies to existing bodies (found via its survey scanner), not new planets or moons.
Can I use EVA construction without owning any robotic parts? Yes. EVA construction mode works on ordinary stock parts too — attaching, detaching, and repositioning isn't limited to Breaking Ground's own part set.
Will installing a DLC break my existing saves? No — both DLCs are additive and safe to install into an existing save; your craft and progress carry over unchanged.
Do the Mission Builder's custom missions require other players to own Making History? Generally yes, since the mission file relies on the Mission Builder's systems and parts to play back correctly.
