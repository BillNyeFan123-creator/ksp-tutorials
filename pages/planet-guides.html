Moho

Radius250 km
Gravity~2.7 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
MoonsNone

Moho is the innermost planet and, despite being small and low-gravity, is one of the hardest bodies in the game to actually reach efficiently. Its orbit is both highly inclined (~7°) and highly eccentric relative to Kerbin's, so a cheap transfer window is rare and a poorly-timed one can cost far more Δv in plane-change alone than the transfer itself.

Once you arrive, landing is easy: low gravity, no atmosphere, and (once captured) you can take your time picking a flat spot. The real budget goes into getting there and matching planes, not the final descent.

Common mistake

Burning for a Moho transfer at a random point in your orbit instead of correcting Moho's orbital inclination near one of the two nodes where your orbit and Moho's orbit cross. Doing the plane change separately (and cheaply, near a node, ideally combined with your ejection burn) instead of fighting it during capture saves an enormous amount of Δv.

Expect extreme day/night temperature swings on the surface — panels and radiators matter more here than almost anywhere else in the system.

Eve

Radius700 km
Gravity~16.7 m/s²
Atmosphere~90 km (thick)
MoonsGilly

Eve is the purple-tinted trap of the Kerbol system. Its thick atmosphere and high gravity make landing comparatively forgiving — parachutes work well low down, and the dense air slows you dramatically before you're anywhere near the surface. Getting back off is the problem.

An Eve ascent vehicle needs a Δv budget that rivals or exceeds a full Kerbin launch, delivered by an engine that still performs at high atmospheric pressure and low altitude — meaning you want high-thrust, high-ISP-at-sea-level engines and a low, wide craft rather than a tall thin one, since Eve's ascent profile fights heavy drag the entire way up. Many players land the entire ascent rocket fueled and ready, then simply light it from the surface rather than trying to build anything on-site.

This is the hardest single ascent in stock KSP

Getting off Eve's surface back to orbit can cost 8,000 m/s of Δv or more depending on your ascent profile and starting altitude — by far the most expensive maneuver in the stock game. Budget for it specifically; don't assume "a bit more than Kerbin" is enough.

Landing at higher elevation (lower atmospheric pressure at the biome you land in) meaningfully reduces the ascent cost, which is why many Eve-return attempts specifically target high ground.

Gilly

Radius~13 km
Gravity~0.049 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moons ofEve

Gilly's gravity is so low it barely qualifies as a landing at all — it's closer to docking with an irregularly-shaped rock. Both landing and takeoff are individually easy in terms of Δv, but the low gravity makes precision hard: a slightly-too-fast approach or a firm RCS tap can send you drifting back off the surface entirely.

Practical tip

Kill your relative velocity almost completely before touching down, and consider anchoring with landing legs' brakes rather than relying on gravity to keep you put. Overshooting your intended landing spot due to residual velocity is the single most common Gilly mistake.

Mun

Radius200 km
Gravity~1.63 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moons ofKerbin

The classic first landing target, and for good reason — it's close, airless (so no aerodynamics to worry about), and has a good mix of flat maria-like plains for easy landings plus cratered, mountainous highlands for players wanting more of a challenge or specific science biomes.

Approach: get into low Mun orbit, pick a landing site with a low-angle descent so you have time to react, and kill horizontal velocity well before you kill vertical velocity — the Mun's terrain is rougher than it looks from orbit, and a fast final descent over broken ground is the most common source of tipped-over landers.

Minmus

Radius60 km
Gravity~0.491 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moons ofKerbin

Minmus is the easiest body in the game to land on and return from, despite orbiting farther out than the Mun. Its low gravity keeps every maneuver cheap, and its large flat "flats" biomes give you an enormous margin for error on your final approach — you can come in shallow, take your time, and still land comfortably within a small patch of level ground.

Recommended as your actual first landing

Even though the Mun is visually closer and more commonly attempted first, Minmus is genuinely cheaper Δv round-trip and far more forgiving on touchdown. If your first lander is underpowered, try Minmus before the Mun.

Duna

Radius320 km
Gravity~2.94 m/s²
Atmosphere~50 km (thin)
MoonsIke

Duna is the classic first interplanetary target, and the Mars comparison is deliberate — a thin atmosphere that's too weak to land on parachutes alone from orbital speed, but plenty useful for aerobraking into capture and for slowing a lander in its final descent phase alongside a modest amount of powered braking.

Approach: aerobrake on arrival to cut capture Δv to almost nothing, then use a combination of parachutes and a short retro-burn for the actual landing. Ascent back to orbit costs meaningfully less than descent-from-transfer because you're only fighting Duna's own gravity, not shedding interplanetary arrival speed.

Common mistake

Relying on parachutes alone the way you would on Kerbin. Duna's atmosphere is thin enough that chutes need to be sized generously and deployed with a powered assist for a safe touchdown, especially at higher-elevation landing sites.

Ike

Radius130 km
Gravity~1.1 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moon ofDuna

Ike is tidally locked to Duna and airless, making it a straightforward Mun-style landing once you're in the Duna system. It's a convenient refueling or staging point for a Duna mission since it shares Duna's sphere of influence and is cheap to reach from Duna orbit.

One practical tip: Ike's low gravity and lack of atmosphere mean an ascent stage sized for Duna landings is comfortably overpowered here — if you're running a combined Duna/Ike mission, don't over-build a separate Ike-specific lander.

Dres

Radius138 km
Gravity~1.13 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
MoonsNone

Dres sits in a lonely, asteroid-belt-like gap between Duna and Jool, and the community has more or less made a running joke of how often it gets skipped ("the forgotten planet"). Its orbit is noticeably inclined relative to the ecliptic, which makes finding a cheap transfer window slightly more annoying than the modest Δv numbers alone would suggest.

Once you're there, though, it's an easy landing — low gravity, no atmosphere, no real hazards. The entire difficulty of Dres is the trip out, not the arrival.

Don't actually forget it

If you're doing a Grand Tour, plan Dres in on the way past rather than as a dedicated special trip — the inclination penalty is smaller if you correct it early, combined with your ejection burn.

Jool

Radius6,000 km
GravityN/A (gas giant)
AtmosphereExtremely thick
Moons5 (Laythe, Vall, Tylo, Bop, Pol)

Jool cannot be landed on — any craft descending into its atmosphere is crushed or destroyed well above where a solid surface would even be, if one exists at all. Treat it purely as a gravity well with five very different moons around it.

Jool's atmosphere is however extremely useful for aerocapture: a well-shielded craft can shed a huge amount of interplanetary arrival speed on a single pass through the upper layers before settling into a Jool-orbiting trajectory, saving a large propulsive capture burn. Just don't go deep enough to get destroyed — margins for error shrink fast here.

Laythe

Radius500 km
Gravity~7.85 m/s²
Atmosphere~50 km + oceans
Moon ofJool

Laythe is the only other stock body with a breathable-pressure atmosphere (not breathable composition) and liquid oceans, making it the go-to "second Kerbin" for spaceplane and parachute landings alike. It's a very popular colonization and challenge target for exactly that reason.

Approach: you can fly it much like Kerbin — wings work, parachutes work, and a runway-style spaceplane landing is entirely viable if you brought one this far. Just remember Laythe's gravity is noticeably lower than Kerbin's, so your approach speeds and stall behavior will differ.

Land near a coastline

Laythe's mix of ocean and land biomes means a coastal landing site gives you easy access to both science biome types without a long overland or oversea trip afterward.

Vall

Radius300 km
Gravity~2.31 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moon ofJool

Vall is icy, airless, and moderate in difficulty — its gravity is high enough that you can't just float down like Pol or Bop, but nowhere near Tylo's brutal numbers. Terrain can be deceptively steep in places, so pick landing sites with the same care you'd use on the Mun.

A standard powered descent with a wide, low lander works fine. Budget a bit more Δv margin here than an airless-body landing might otherwise suggest, purely because of local terrain unpredictability.

Tylo

Radius600 km
Gravity~7.85 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moon ofJool

Tylo is the trap of the Jool system: it's the same size as Kerbin and has Kerbin-comparable surface gravity, but zero atmosphere. That means no aerobraking on the way in and no parachutes on the way down — every m/s of your descent and every m/s of your ascent has to come from your engines.

Plan for a Kerbin-liftoff-grade lander

A Tylo lander needs a Δv budget and TWR comparable to a full Kerbin launch, both for landing and for getting back to orbit — roughly 2,800+ m/s each way. Underestimating Tylo because "it's just another moon" is one of the most common reasons a Jool-5 attempt fails.

Because of this, most successful Tylo landers are purpose-built and separate from whatever handles the system's other, much cheaper moons.

Bop

Gravity~0.589 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moon ofJool
ShapeIrregular

Bop is small, irregularly shaped (it reads like a captured asteroid), and sits on a tumbling, eccentric orbit around Jool. Its low gravity makes landing and ascent cheap, but the irregular terrain and orbit mean your approach planning benefits from extra patience — don't rush the rendezvous.

Pol

Gravity~0.373 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
Moon ofJool
TerrainCratered, small

Pol is generally considered the easiest of Jool's five moons to land on — very low gravity, no atmosphere, and gently cratered terrain with few surprises. If you're sequencing a Jool-5 attempt, many players tackle Pol first to bank an easy win before moving on to Tylo.

Eeloo

Radius210 km
Gravity~1.69 m/s²
AtmosphereNone
MoonsNone

Eeloo is the farthest stock celestial body from Kerbol — an icy, dwarf-planet-like world with an eccentric, inclined orbit that makes for a long transfer time and a somewhat fiddly window to find. It's commonly treated as the last stop on a Grand Tour, both symbolically and because its awkward orbit is easiest to plan for once you already have a mission clock full of time-warp experience.

Once you arrive, the landing itself is easy — low gravity, no atmosphere, no unusual hazards. All the difficulty is in the trip, not the touchdown.

Suggested first "grand tour" order

If this is your first time stringing multiple destinations together, a sensible order is: Mun and Minmus first to build core landing/return skills, then Duna as your first interplanetary trip, then Dres or Eve depending on whether you want an easy inclined-orbit trip or a serious ascent challenge, then the Jool system (Pol and Bop first, Laythe and Vall next, Tylo last), and finally Moho and Eeloo, since both have the trickiest orbital geometry in the game and benefit most from experience you'll have built up by then.
